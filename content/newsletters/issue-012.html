📷 Convention Corner

Mastering Long Exposure Photography

Part 6: Getting the Focus Right

You can get the exposure perfect, choose the ideal shutter speed and capture beautifully smooth water, but if the important parts of the photograph aren't sharp, there's very little you can do afterwards to rescue the image.

During his VAPS Convention presentation, Ian van der Wolde spent some time discussing focus because it was something he had learned through experience with his own early long exposure photographs.

The good news is that achieving accurate focus isn't particularly difficult. The trick is to do it before the ND filter goes on the camera.

Read Part 6: Getting the Focus Right

Why ND Filters Make Focusing Difficult

A strong ND filter dramatically reduces the amount of light reaching the camera.

A 10-stop filter, for example, allows only about 1/1000th of the original light through.

That's exactly what we want when we're trying to turn a normal exposure into a long exposure, but it can make focusing considerably more difficult.

Depending on the camera and conditions, autofocus may struggle or fail completely once a strong ND filter is fitted.

Ian's solution is straightforward:

Compose and focus first. Add the ND filter afterwards.

Start Without the Filter

Before fitting the ND filter:

  1. Set up the tripod.
  2. Compose the photograph.
  3. Focus carefully.
  4. Check the focus at high magnification.
  5. Switch to manual focus if necessary.
  6. Only then attach the ND filter.

Once you've established focus, the objective is to make sure nothing changes.

Be particularly careful when screwing a filter onto the lens. It's surprisingly easy to accidentally move the focusing ring while fitting the filter.

If you're using a filter holder, slide the filter into position carefully without disturbing the camera.

Don't Just Trust the Autofocus Confirmation

Ian explained that some of his early long exposure images weren't as sharp as he expected.

He had been relying on autofocus.

His approach changed to manually checking focus using the camera's Live View display.

With a DSLR, Ian recommended switching to Live View, magnifying the image on the rear screen to 100%, and manually adjusting focus until the detail visibly snaps into focus.

He also demonstrated a focusing loupe that can be placed over the rear LCD. Apart from magnifying the display, the loupe blocks surrounding light, making the screen much easier to examine outdoors.

Mirrorless Cameras Make This Easier

One advantage of modern mirrorless cameras is the electronic viewfinder.

You can magnify part of the scene inside the viewfinder and inspect critical focus without surrounding daylight making the image difficult to see.

Many mirrorless cameras also offer focus peaking, which highlights areas the camera believes are in focus.

These tools are extremely useful, but the principle remains the same:

Check the focus yourself rather than simply assuming the camera has got it right.

What Should You Focus On?

This depends entirely on the photograph.

It isn't automatically infinity.

If the photograph contains an important foreground object, simply focusing on the horizon may leave that foreground noticeably soft.

Likewise, focusing on something immediately in front of the camera may sacrifice distant detail.

For landscapes containing both foreground and distant subjects, consider where the available depth of field needs to fall through the scene.

Ian often works around apertures such as f/11, where sufficient depth of field can be achieved while retaining excellent image quality.

The important thing is to make a deliberate decision about where you want maximum sharpness, rather than simply turning the lens to infinity and hoping everything will be sharp.

Image Stabilisation: Check Your Camera

Ian also recommended turning image stabilisation off when working from a tripod.

That has traditionally been good advice because some stabilisation systems can attempt to compensate for movement that isn't actually occurring, potentially introducing blur during a long exposure.

However, camera technology has changed considerably.

Some modern cameras and lenses automatically recognise when they're mounted on a tripod, while manufacturers of some systems recommend leaving particular stabilisation modes active.

So rather than applying one rule to every camera:

Check the manufacturer's instructions for your particular camera and lens.

The objective is the same regardless of the system: eliminate anything that could introduce unwanted movement during the exposure.

Check Again Before Starting a Very Long Exposure

This becomes increasingly important as exposure times grow.

If you're about to make a 30-second exposure, checking focus takes only a few moments.

If you're about to make a 10-minute exposure followed by another 10 minutes of long exposure noise reduction, discovering afterwards that the image is out of focus becomes considerably more frustrating.

Before opening the shutter, run through a quick mental checklist:

Composition. Focus. Stability. Exposure. Filter. Light leaks.

Then take the photograph.

Don't Forget the Tripod

Accurate focus and camera stability go hand in hand.

You can focus perfectly and still produce a soft photograph if the camera moves during the exposure.

Once focus is established:

  • Avoid touching the camera.
  • Make sure the tripod is stable.
  • Secure any loose remote-release cables.
  • Be careful on boardwalks, bridges and other surfaces that can vibrate.
  • Allow the camera to settle after fitting the filter.
  • Use a remote release or suitable shutter delay.

These little details become increasingly important as exposures become longer.

Ian's Simple Focusing Workflow

For anyone beginning with long exposure photography, Ian's approach can be reduced to a very simple sequence:

Compose → Focus → Magnify → Check → Manual Focus → Add Filter → Expose

It's worth turning that into a habit.

After a while, you'll perform the sequence automatically.

Try It This Week

You don't even need an ND filter for this week's exercise.

Put your camera on a tripod and choose a landscape containing both near and distant detail.

Focus as you normally would and take a photograph.

Now switch to Live View or your electronic viewfinder and magnify an important part of the scene as far as your camera allows.

Manually refine the focus.

Take another photograph.

Back at the computer, compare the two images at 100%.

Can you see a difference?

Then repeat the exercise at several different focusing distances and observe what happens to the foreground and background.

It's a simple exercise, but understanding exactly where your camera is focusing and how depth of field extends around that point is an important skill for every landscape photographer, not just those shooting long exposures.

Quick Tip

Focus before fitting a strong ND filter.

Once you've established critical focus, switch to manual focus so the camera doesn't attempt to refocus when you press the shutter.

Then carefully fit the ND filter without touching the focusing ring.

It's one small habit that can save a very long wait for a very disappointing photograph.

💻 Editing Corner

Lightroom Basics – Part 5

Sharpening Without Halos

Sharpening is one of those Lightroom adjustments that can improve almost every photograph, but it is also very easy to overdo.

The aim of sharpening is not to make an out-of-focus photograph sharp. Instead, sharpening increases contrast along existing edges so that detail appears clearer and more defined.

Too much sharpening can produce obvious bright or dark outlines around edges, commonly called halos. It can also make noise, skin texture and backgrounds look harsh.

Read Lightroom Basics Part 5: Sharpening Without Halos

A Simple Lightroom Sharpening Process

1. Zoom to 100%

Open the photograph in Lightroom Classic's Develop module and zoom to 1:1 or 100%.

Choose an area containing useful fine detail, such as:

  • Eyes and eyelashes in a portrait.
  • Feathers on a bird.
  • Fur.
  • Tree bark.
  • Rock texture.
  • Lettering or architectural detail.

Do not judge sharpening while viewing the entire photograph fitted to the screen.

2. Open the Detail Panel

In Lightroom Classic, open the Detail panel.

Under Sharpening you will find four main controls:

  • Amount
  • Radius
  • Detail
  • Masking

Each performs a different job.

Amount – How Much Sharpening?

The Amount slider controls the overall strength of the sharpening effect.

Moving it to the right increases the contrast Lightroom applies around edges. Setting Amount to 0 switches sharpening off.

Start modestly and increase it until the important detail looks clearer.

Then back it off slightly if the result begins to look harsh.

Beginner rule: If you can obviously see the sharpening effect, you may already have gone too far.

Radius – How Wide Are the Sharpened Edges?

Radius controls the width of the area around an edge affected by sharpening.

Fine-detail photographs generally suit a smaller Radius, while photographs containing larger details can tolerate a larger setting. A Radius that is too large can make the result look unnatural.

For beginners, there is rarely a need to make large Radius adjustments. Small movements are usually enough.

Detail – How Much Fine Texture?

The Detail slider determines how much fine texture Lightroom attempts to sharpen.

Lower settings concentrate mainly on edges.

Higher settings emphasise more fine detail and texture.

That can be useful for subjects such as feathers, fur, rocks and detailed landscapes, but high Detail settings can also exaggerate noise and unwanted texture.

For portraits, particularly skin, a more restrained Detail setting is usually preferable.

Masking – The Beginner's Best Friend

Of all the sharpening controls, Masking is probably the most useful one for beginners.

At 0, Lightroom applies sharpening throughout virtually the entire photograph.

As Masking approaches 100, sharpening becomes increasingly restricted to the strongest edges.

This means you can sharpen the important edges without unnecessarily sharpening smooth areas such as:

  • Blue skies.
  • Blurred backgrounds.
  • Smooth water.
  • Skin.
  • Out-of-focus areas.

See Exactly What Masking Is Doing

Hold Alt on Windows or Option on Mac while moving the Masking slider.

Lightroom displays a temporary black-and-white preview:

  • White areas will be sharpened.
  • Black areas will be protected from sharpening.

For a bird photograph, for example, you might increase Masking until the bird's edges and feather detail remain white while most of the smooth sky becomes black.

That allows the bird to be sharpened without also making noise in the sky more obvious.

Watch for Halos

One of the clearest signs of excessive sharpening is a bright or dark line appearing along a high-contrast edge.

Look closely around:

  • Buildings against the sky.
  • Tree branches.
  • Bird outlines.
  • Mountain ridges.
  • Dark objects against bright backgrounds.

If you see halos, reduce Amount, Radius, or both.

Sharpening Does Not Fix Missed Focus

Sharpening can improve the appearance of detail that already exists.

It cannot restore detail that the camera never captured.

If the subject is badly out of focus or heavily affected by camera movement, simply increasing sharpening will usually create a harsher-looking blurry photograph rather than a genuinely sharp one.

Knowing when to stop is part of good editing.

A Simple Beginner Method

  • Zoom to 100%.
  • Open the Detail panel.
  • Increase Amount gradually.
  • Keep Radius modest.
  • Use Detail carefully.
  • Hold Alt/Option while increasing Masking.
  • Concentrate sharpening on the important edges.
  • Check carefully for halos.
  • Compare with sharpening temporarily reduced or switched off.

There is no single correct set of numbers because different photographs contain different amounts and types of detail.

Final Check

Before moving on, ask:

  • Does the important subject look clearer?
  • Have fine details improved without becoming crunchy?
  • Are smooth areas still smooth?
  • Have I made noise more obvious?
  • Can I see bright or dark halos around edges?
  • Does the photograph still look natural?

If you are unsure, reduce the sharpening slightly.

Remember

Sharpen the subject, not every pixel.

Masking is often the easiest way to achieve that.

📸 Photographer's Tips

🌱 Beginner Tip – B008

Understand What ISO Does

ISO controls how sensitive your camera is to the light being recorded.

A lower ISO, such as ISO 100, will generally give you the cleanest image quality. Raising the ISO allows you to use a faster shutter speed or shoot in lower light, but it can also introduce more visible noise into the photograph.

Don't be afraid to raise your ISO when you need to. A sharp photograph at ISO 1600 is usually far more useful than a blurry photograph at ISO 100.

Try photographing the same scene at several ISO settings and compare the results on your computer.

📷 Intermediate Tip – I008

Use the Direction of Movement

When photographing a person, animal, vehicle or anything else moving across the frame, consider leaving more space in front of the subject than behind it.

This gives the subject somewhere to move into and often makes the composition feel more natural.

The same principle can apply to where a person or animal is looking. Leaving space in the direction of their gaze can help guide the viewer through the photograph.

Of course, breaking this convention can create tension, but it works best when you do it deliberately.

⭐ Advanced Tip – A008

Look for Tonal Separation

Two objects can be completely different colours yet merge together if they have similar brightness.

When composing an image, consider not only colour but also tone. A dark subject against a dark background may lose definition, while placing that same subject against a lighter area can immediately create separation.

One useful technique is to imagine the scene in black and white. If your subject still stands out clearly without colour, you probably have good tonal separation.

This becomes particularly important when creating monochrome images, where differences in brightness must do much of the work that colour normally provides.

📷 This Week's Camera Challenge

Make the Ordinary Interesting

One thing our World Photography Day photographs showed is that you don't need to travel somewhere spectacular to find something worth photographing.

A flower, breakfast, a snail, wildlife close to home or something you notice while going about your normal day can all become worthwhile photographs if you stop and look carefully.

This week's challenge: Photograph something you would normally walk straight past.

It might be something around the house, in the backyard, on your morning walk, at work or somewhere you visit regularly.

The challenge is not simply to record it. Try to make an interesting photograph from an ordinary subject.

Think about changing your viewpoint, moving closer, using light and shadow, simplifying the background, looking for colour, shape, texture or pattern, or trying the photograph in black and white.

Before pressing the shutter, ask yourself:

“What made me notice this?”

Then try to make that the strongest part of your photograph.

You don't need special equipment. Your camera or phone is perfectly adequate.

Sometimes improving your photography isn't about finding better subjects. It's about getting better at seeing the subjects already around you.
