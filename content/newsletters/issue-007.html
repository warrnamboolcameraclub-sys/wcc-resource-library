✂️ Workshop Corner

Mat Cutting Workshop – Learning the Art of Presentation

Our first Mat Cutting Workshop was recently held, giving members the opportunity to learn the skills required to professionally prepare prints for competition.

Read the mat cutting workshop update

The smaller group created an ideal learning environment, allowing plenty of one-on-one instruction and hands-on time with the club's professional mat cutter. Members worked through measuring borders, cutting mats accurately and mounting their images using techniques that produce a clean, professional presentation.

Print presentation plays an important role in competition success, and these workshops are designed to give members the confidence to prepare their own entries rather than relying on others.

To allow more members to take advantage of this practical session, we'll be holding another workshop during the week beginning 3 August, most likely on Monday or Wednesday evening. The final date will be confirmed shortly.

If you've been thinking about entering print competitions, this is a great opportunity to learn new skills in a relaxed and supportive environment.

📋 Competition Rules Update

Thank you to everyone who took the time to review the proposed Competition Rules and provide constructive feedback.

The committee received a wide range of thoughtful suggestions and comments, resulting in a number of refinements to improve both the clarity and practicality of the rules.

Read the Competition Rules update

The quality of feedback received reflects the passion our members have for ensuring competitions remain fair, enjoyable and educational.

One of the more significant discussions centred around the use of Artificial Intelligence in photography. Rather than introducing restrictive rules now before we have practical experience, I think it may be better to look at them as advisory. This is just my view as I feel that this is moving very quickly and not a lot of clubs have adopted any type of rule as yet.

The members may well decide to have them as official rules next year and that is fine. I feel that by having advisory rules it gives everyone a chance to observe how AI-assisted editing tools are used within the club, which will help us provide guidance where required and gather real-world experience before considering any future actual rule changes.

Thank you again to everyone who contributed. Your feedback has helped produce a stronger set of rules that will continue to support our competitions into the future.

I have updated some of the rules given the feedback and given the discussion at the last committee meeting. Please take the chance to look at the changes and continue to provide feedback. Please take time to read the attached update.

📷 Convention Corner

Ethical Bird Photography Series – Article 3

The Four P's: Finding Beauty in Everyday Behaviour

Based on a presentation by Kim Wormald at the 2026 VAPS Convention

In the last article in this series, we explored The Four F's—behaviours that ethical photographers should avoid encouraging or manipulating in pursuit of a dramatic image.

This time, we turn our attention to a far more rewarding approach.

Read The Four P's: Finding Beauty in Everyday Behaviour

Rather than chasing action, Kim encouraged photographers to embrace The Four P's:

  • Perching
  • Preening
  • Posing
  • Portraits

At first glance, these subjects may sound ordinary. They certainly won't generate the same excitement as a bird in flight or a dramatic territorial battle. But that's exactly the point.

Some of the most memorable wildlife photographs are those that capture an animal simply being itself.

A bird quietly perched on a branch can reveal its preferred habitat, the quality of the light and the environment it calls home. A preening bird demonstrates trust and contentment, often allowing photographers to observe fascinating behaviour that many people never notice. A natural pose can communicate confidence, curiosity or alertness, while a carefully composed portrait showcases the remarkable detail, colour and texture of feathers, along with the character reflected in the bird's eyes.

These images aren't created by forcing behaviour. They are earned through patience, careful observation and an understanding of wildlife.

Sometimes, simply waiting allows nature to provide something extraordinary.

The accompanying image of the Reddish Egret (borrowed from the internet just as an example) is a perfect example. At first glance it appears to be a spectacular action photograph. In reality, the bird is simply displaying its natural hunting behaviour, running through shallow water with wings spread to confuse and trap fish. The photographer didn't create the moment—they recognised the behaviour, anticipated what was about to happen and were ready when it unfolded.

Likewise, a portrait can often tell a story without any action at all. The accompanying image of the Canada Goose (also borrowed from the internet), captures a moment of curiosity and personality. There is no dramatic behaviour, yet the image invites the viewer to pause and connect with the subject.

As Kim reminded us throughout her presentation, ethical wildlife photography isn't about creating spectacular moments—it's about recognising them when nature provides them.

Takeaway

The next time you're photographing wildlife, spend more time watching than shooting. Look for moments of calm, natural behaviour and ask yourself, "What story is this animal telling me right now?" You may discover that the simplest moments often produce the most meaningful photographs.

Thank you to Kim Wormald for sharing her knowledge and passion for ethical wildlife photography. We hope this three-part series has inspired you to slow down, observe more carefully and appreciate the beauty of natural behaviour.

📸 Photographer's Tips

🌱 Beginner Tip (B003)

Choose a Single Focus Point

Many cameras default to automatically choosing where to focus, but they don't always pick the subject you intended.

Instead, select a single focus point and place it over the most important part of your subject—usually the eye when photographing wildlife, birds or people.

Taking control of your focus point is one of the quickest ways to produce consistently sharper images.

📷 Intermediate Tip (I003)

Use Foreground Interest to Add Depth

Landscape photographs can sometimes appear flat because everything seems to be the same distance from the viewer.

Look for rocks, flowers, driftwood or other interesting objects to include in the foreground. These elements help draw the viewer into the scene and create a stronger sense of depth and scale.

Just be sure the foreground complements the image rather than competing with your main subject.

⭐ Advanced Tip (A003)

Wait for the Right Moment

The difference between a good photograph and an outstanding one is often timing.

Rather than taking the first image you see, spend a little longer observing the scene. Wait for the person to enter the frame, the bird to turn towards the camera, the wave to break, or the light to fall exactly where you want it.

Experienced photographers often create memorable images not because they take more photographs, but because they recognise the decisive moment—and have the patience to wait for it.

🎯 Camera Challenge

Capture the Landscape You Know Best

With Craig Richards visiting the club in August, this fortnight's challenge is to photograph a landscape close to home.

It doesn't have to be an iconic location. In fact, the challenge is to find beauty in somewhere familiar—whether it's the coastline, a country road, a favourite beach, a local river, a stand of old trees or even a quiet corner of your own neighbourhood.

As you compose your image, think about:

  • Strong foreground interest.
  • The quality and direction of the light.
  • Leading lines that guide the viewer through the scene.
  • Simplicity and clean composition.
  • Waiting for the right moment rather than settling for the first photograph.

Bring your favourite image along to Craig's presentation or workshop and see how his ideas might inspire you to look at the same scene differently next time.
