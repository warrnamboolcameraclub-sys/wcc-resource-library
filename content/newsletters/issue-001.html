🏠 From the Editor

Hi everyone,

I hope everyone enjoyed Thursday night's discussion on photographic judging and the CAT (Concept, Aesthetic and Technical) framework.

🏆 Last Thursday's Meeting

Last Thursday's presentation on photographic judging certainly had people talking.

After exploring Theo van der Meulen's CAT (Concept, Aesthetic and Technical) judging framework, members were divided into three groups. Each group was asked to assess a photograph against just one component of the framework before rotating to judge the remaining categories.

By the end of the exercise every group had independently assessed the image for Concept, Aesthetic and Technical merit.

What made the exercise particularly interesting was that, despite the discussions and differing opinions around the room, the final scores from the three groups were remarkably close, ranging from 12 to 14 out of 15.

The exercise generated plenty of discussion and demonstrated how the CAT framework encourages photographers to think more deeply about why an image works rather than simply looking for technical faults.

The evening was so well received that the committee has agreed to approach VAPS and APJA to explore whether Warrnambool Camera Club could become involved in providing panel judging for other clubs.

The exercise demonstrated the value of multiple photographers assessing an image from different perspectives and generated some excellent discussion throughout the evening.

We look forward to exploring the idea further and will keep members updated as discussions progress.

A big thank you to everyone who participated and contributed to the discussion.

Members who would like to revisit the material discussed on the night can find a PDF copy of the presentation attached to this email.

📷 Convention Corner

Long Exposure: Serenity Through the Lens

Lessons from Ian van der Wolde's VAPS Convention Presentation

One of the standout presentations at this year's VAPS Convention was Melbourne photographer Ian van der Wolde's engaging session on long exposure photography.

Ian recently celebrated 40 years as a professional photographer and shared not only the technical aspects of long exposure photography but also the creative philosophy behind it. While many photographers associate long exposure work with silky water and blurred clouds, Ian demonstrated that the real power of the technique lies in its ability to simplify a scene and create atmosphere.

Throughout the presentation he showed how ordinary locations can be transformed into extraordinary images through careful observation, patience and deliberate use of shutter speed. Many of his award-winning photographs were taken not in far-flung destinations but at local Victorian beaches, piers and coastal locations that most people would simply walk past.

Perhaps the strongest message from the presentation was that photography should remain enjoyable. Ian spoke openly about rediscovering his passion through personal projects and reminded us that curiosity, experimentation and continued learning are essential ingredients for photographic growth.

Over the coming weeks we will be exploring many of the ideas from Ian's presentation in a new educational series designed to help members better understand long exposure photography and how it can be used creatively.

📷 Mastering Long Exposure Photography

Part 1: Why Long Exposure?

When most photographers think about long exposure photography, they think about smooth water, streaking clouds and dramatic seascapes. While these effects can certainly be beautiful, they are only part of the story.

At its heart, long exposure photography is about controlling time.

A fast shutter speed freezes a moment. A long shutter speed records what happens over a period of time. Instead of showing a fraction of a second, the camera records seconds, minutes, or even longer, blending movement into a single image.

This ability to compress time allows photographers to create images that often feel calmer and more atmospheric than the original scene.

During his presentation, Ian showed an example of a coastal scene filled with birds, choppy water and visual activity. Through the use of a long exposure, the final image became quiet, minimalist and serene. The distractions disappeared, allowing the viewer to focus on the composition itself.

One of the most important lessons from the presentation was that long exposure should not be viewed as a special effect. Instead, it should be considered another creative tool available to photographers.

Ask yourself:

  • Does a longer shutter speed improve the image?
  • Does it simplify the scene?
  • Does it strengthen the mood?
  • Does it help communicate what attracted you to the scene in the first place?

Sometimes the answer will be yes.

Sometimes a frozen wave, dramatic cloud formation or rapidly moving subject may actually look stronger with a faster shutter speed.

The key is understanding that shutter speed is a creative decision rather than simply a technical setting.

A Note About ND Filters

Many long exposure images are created using Neutral Density (ND) filters.

An ND filter works like a pair of sunglasses for your camera, reducing the amount of light entering the lens and allowing longer shutter speeds to be used during daylight hours.

Without an ND filter, achieving exposures of 10, 20 or 30 seconds in bright daylight is usually impossible.

Don't worry if you don't own an ND filter. You can still experiment with long exposures at dawn, dusk, after sunset, in shaded areas or on heavily overcast days when naturally slower shutter speeds are available.

We will look at ND filters in more detail later in this series.

📸 Try It This Week

Find a local beach, river, lake, fountain or waterfall and photograph the same scene using a range of shutter speeds.

Try:

  • 1/30 second
  • 1/4 second
  • 1 second
  • 2 seconds

If you own an ND filter, continue experimenting with longer exposures of 5, 10 or 30 seconds.

Compare the results and ask yourself:

"At what point does the image begin to feel calmer?"

There is no right answer.

The goal is simply to start thinking about shutter speed as a creative choice.

📋 Competition Rules Review

The committee is currently reviewing the club competition rules to ensure they remain relevant and reflect modern photographic practices.

Rather than trying to cover this important topic in a brief newsletter article, we will be distributing a separate communication in the coming weeks outlining the proposed changes and inviting feedback from all members before any decisions are made.

We encourage members to take the time to review the proposal when it is released and to share their thoughts as part of the consultation process.
