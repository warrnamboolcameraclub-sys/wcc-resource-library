📷 Running Festival Photography Guide

We have developed a mobile field guide for Club photographers positioned around the Running Festival course.

The idea is to give everyone a simple reference they can use before the weekend and while they are out on the course.

Practice This Week

Warrnambool parkrun at Lake Pertobe on Saturday morning is an ideal opportunity to practise runners coming toward the camera.

Try staying in one position, working in short bursts, shooting both horizontal and vertical frames and paying attention to the background.

Recommended Starting Settings
  • Manual mode
  • 1/1000 sec
  • f/8
  • Auto ISO
  • Continuous autofocus
  • Tracking / Zone / Wide AF
  • Continuous Low or Medium drive
  • Auto White Balance
  • Evaluative / Matrix / Multi metering
  • Stabilisation On if available

These are starting points, not rules. The aim is to give photographers a dependable setup that can then be adjusted for the conditions and their own camera.

Why f/8?

f/8 gives useful depth of field, helps with groups and provides some tolerance if autofocus is slightly behind a runner approaching the camera. Many lenses also perform very strongly around this aperture.

File Format and Time Sync

For this event we want JPEG only, not RAW and not RAW + JPEG. Use Medium JPEG, Fine / High Quality and sRGB.

With many photographers potentially producing thousands of files, this makes copying, uploading and sorting much quicker and avoids a RAW-processing step.

Also make sure your camera date and time are synchronised to your phone or watch as closely as practical, ideally within one second. This allows files from multiple photographers to be merged chronologically later.

Shooting Tips
  • Track the runner early.
  • Use short bursts of around 2–4 frames.
  • Leave some space in front of the runner.
  • Watch the background.
  • Look for faces and expression.
  • Vary singles, pairs and groups.
  • Use the environment and tell the story of the event.
  • If continuous AF struggles, pre-focus on a fixed point and photograph the runner as they reach it.
  • Once you have safe sharp shots, you can experiment with panning around 1/60–1/125 sec.

Safety Comes First

The runner always has right of way. Never step into the course to get a photograph.

Think Beyond the Tight Action Shot

Not every photograph needs to fill the frame with a runner. At the right location, aim for runner + course + Warrnambool. Those environmental photographs help tell the story of where the event took place.

📷 Convention Corner

This week in Mastering Long Exposure Photography, Ian van der Wolde explains why starting around your camera’s base ISO can improve image quality while helping you achieve the longer shutter speeds you are deliberately looking for.

Mastering Long Exposure Photography – Part 8: Base ISO – Why Lower Can Be Better for Long Exposures

Mastering Long Exposure Photography

Part 8: Base ISO – Why Lower Can Be Better for Long Exposures

You’re standing beside the ocean as the light begins to fade.

Your shutter speed is getting longer. For everyday photography, the instinct might be to increase the ISO so you can keep shooting at a faster shutter speed.

But during his VAPS Convention presentation, Ian van der Wolde suggested thinking in exactly the opposite direction when shooting long exposures.

After all, if we’re deliberately trying to make the exposure longer, why increase the ISO to make it shorter?

For Ian, the starting point is generally the camera’s base ISO, giving him excellent image quality while allowing the shutter to stay open longer.

First Things First – What Does ISO Mean?

ISO, pronounced “eye-so”, takes its name from the International Organization for Standardization, the body responsible for international standards covering thousands of products and processes.

Interestingly, ISO isn’t an acronym formed from the organisation’s English name. ISO was chosen as its universal short name, derived from the Greek word isos, meaning equal.

So when we’re talking photography, you would normally say:

“eye-so one hundred”

rather than spelling out “I-S-O one hundred”.

In photography, ISO numbers form part of a standardised system for expressing photographic sensitivity/exposure response. On a digital camera, changing the ISO affects how the camera uses the signal captured by the sensor.

For photographers, the practical effect is straightforward:

Raise the ISO and you can use a faster shutter speed. Lower the ISO and, all else being equal, you need a longer shutter speed.

And that second option is particularly interesting for long exposure photography.

What is Base ISO?

Your camera has a base ISO at which its sensor and electronics provide their fundamental performance characteristics.

Depending on the camera, this might be ISO 64, ISO 100, ISO 200 or another value.

Ian stressed the importance of knowing your own camera rather than simply following a generic setting.

There’s another little trap here.

Base ISO isn’t necessarily the lowest ISO number your camera allows you to select.

Some cameras offer extended settings such as LOW or ISO 50 even though their base ISO may be ISO 100.

So don’t simply turn the ISO dial as low as it will go.

Find out the base ISO for your particular camera.

Why Start at Base ISO?

There are two particularly good reasons when shooting long exposures.

The first is image quality.

Working around base ISO generally gives you excellent image quality and strong dynamic-range performance. That’s particularly useful in landscape photography, where we may be trying to retain information from a relatively bright sky right through to a much darker foreground.

The second reason is just as important:

Lower ISO helps us achieve longer shutter speeds.

And that’s precisely what we’re trying to do.

Let’s See What Happens

Suppose our starting exposure is:

ISO 400 • f/11 • 1 second

Reduce the ISO by one stop and compensate by doubling the exposure time:

ISO 200 • f/11 • 2 seconds

Reduce it another stop:

ISO 100 • f/11 • 4 seconds

The overall exposure remains equivalent, but something important has changed.

Time.

Anything moving through the photograph now has longer to move. Water becomes smoother. Waves begin to blur together. Clouds travel further across the frame. Moving people may become increasingly blurred.

We’ve used ISO as part of our creative control over movement.

But Aren’t Modern Cameras Brilliant at High ISO?

They are.

Modern cameras can produce remarkably good photographs at ISO settings that would have been problematic with earlier digital cameras.

There’s nothing inherently wrong with using ISO 800, 1600 or considerably higher when the photograph requires it.

If you’re photographing sport, wildlife or anything else where you need a fast shutter speed, increasing ISO can be exactly the right decision.

But long exposure photography is different.

The camera is normally securely mounted on a tripod and we’re deliberately allowing movement to occur.

We don’t need the higher ISO to give us a fast shutter speed.

We actually want the opposite.

Don’t Confuse Low ISO with Correct Exposure

There’s an important connection here with Part 7 and our Understanding the Histogram tip sheet.

Selecting ISO 100 doesn’t automatically guarantee a clean, high-quality photograph.

If you badly underexpose the image and then try to recover very dark shadows during processing, noise can still become visible.

That’s why Ian’s advice about watching the histogram remains important.

We’re trying to capture a well-exposed RAW file containing as much useful information as practical while protecting important highlights.

So the rule isn’t:

Always use the lowest ISO.

A much better approach is:

Start around your camera’s base ISO and make a technically sound exposure.

Now Add the ND Filter

This is where several lessons from our Long Exposure series begin fitting together.

We’ve:

  • Mounted the camera securely on our tripod
  • Composed the photograph
  • Focused before fitting the filter
  • Selected our base ISO
  • Chosen an appropriate aperture
  • Made a test exposure
  • Checked the histogram

Now we can introduce the ND filter we discussed in Part 5.

Suppose our correct unfiltered exposure at ISO 100 and f/11 is 1/125 second.

Add a 10-stop ND filter and the equivalent exposure becomes approximately 8 seconds.

We’ve gone from an almost instantaneous exposure to one capable of dramatically changing the appearance of moving water.

And if the natural light is already lower, our exposure can quickly extend into tens of seconds or several minutes.

Longer Isn’t Automatically Better

This was another important message running through Ian’s presentation.

Long exposure photography isn’t a competition to see who can leave their shutter open the longest.

A two-second exposure may create beautiful texture in moving water. Thirty seconds might turn it into something completely different. Five minutes could remove virtually all of that texture.

None is automatically better than the others.

The shutter speed should serve the photograph.

Ian’s work demonstrates just how dramatically controlling time can change the mood of an image, but the creative decision still belongs to the photographer.

Know Your Camera Before You Leave Home

Ian repeatedly stressed the importance of becoming familiar with your equipment.

Long exposure photography frequently happens in darkness, cold weather or rapidly changing light. That’s not when you want to discover that you don’t know how to change a setting.

Before your next outing, find the answers to these questions:

  • What is my camera’s base ISO?
  • Does it have extended LOW ISO settings?
  • How quickly can I change ISO?
  • How does changing ISO affect my shutter speed in Manual mode?
  • How do I display the histogram?
  • How do I activate highlight warnings?

Learning those controls at home makes life considerably easier when you’re standing beside the ocean in the dark.

📷 Try It This Week

You need only a tripod and something that moves for this exercise. Running water is ideal, although moving clouds, traffic or people can also work.

Set the camera to Manual exposure and choose an aperture such as f/8 or f/11.

Establish a correct exposure at ISO 800. Take a photograph. Now reduce the ISO one stop and double the shutter speed each time to maintain equivalent exposure.

ISOShutter Speed
800½ second
4001 second
2002 seconds
1004 seconds

Your actual starting shutter speed will depend on the available light, but the relationship remains the same.

Now compare the photographs.

Don’t concentrate solely on noise or sharpness. Look at the movement.

What happened to the water? What happened to the clouds? Did people begin to blur or disappear? Which shutter speed created the photograph you actually prefer?

That’s the important question.

You’ve now used ISO not merely as a technical camera setting, but as part of your creative control over time and movement.

💡 Quick Tip

For long exposure photography, start around your camera’s base ISO rather than automatically increasing ISO because it’s getting dark.

Higher ISO helps us obtain faster shutter speeds. Base ISO allows us to retain excellent image quality while encouraging the longer shutter speeds we’re deliberately trying to achieve.

And remember: ISO is pronounced “eye-so”.

💻 Editing Corner

This week in Lightroom Basics we move from adjustments that affect the whole photograph to local adjustments, using masks to change only the area that needs attention.

Lightroom Basics – Part 7: Local Adjustments – Brushes & Masks

Local Adjustments: Brushes & Masks

So far in this Lightroom Basics series, most of the adjustments we have looked at affect the whole photograph.

But what if only one part of the image needs changing?

Perhaps the subject needs to be a little brighter, the sky is too bright, or the background is competing with the main subject.

This is where Masks become extremely useful.

The important beginner principle is simple:

Use a mask when you want to change one part of the photograph without changing everything else.

1. Open the Masking Panel
  1. Open the photograph in the Develop module.
  2. Select Masking from the tool strip.
  3. Choose the type of mask you want to create.

Lightroom displays the selected area as an overlay, usually red, so you can see exactly which part of the photograph will be affected.

Start With Select Subject

For beginners, Select Subject is one of the easiest masking tools to understand.

Choose Masking → Select Subject.

Lightroom analyses the photograph and attempts to identify the main subject automatically.

For example, if you have photographed a bird against a darker background, Lightroom may create a mask covering just the bird.

You could then:

  • Increase Exposure slightly
  • Open the Shadows
  • Add a little Texture
  • Adjust colour
  • Make the subject slightly warmer or cooler

The background remains unchanged. This is often much more effective than brightening the entire photograph.

Select Sky

For landscape photography, Select Sky can save a considerable amount of time.

Choose Masking → Select Sky.

Lightroom automatically analyses the photograph and creates a mask over the sky.

You might then reduce Exposure or Highlights, add a little Dehaze, adjust colour temperature, or change saturation.

Beginner warning: A dramatic sky does not necessarily mean a better photograph. If the sky begins looking much darker or more colourful than the rest of the scene, reduce the adjustment.

Select Background

Lightroom can also automatically identify the Background.

This can be useful when you want to separate the subject from its surroundings. You might slightly darken the background, reduce its saturation, reduce Texture or Clarity, or cool the background slightly.

Again, subtlety is important. The viewer should notice the subject, not the fact that you darkened everything around it.

Using the Brush

Sometimes Lightroom’s automatic selections do not cover exactly what you need. That is where the Brush becomes useful.

Choose Masking → Brush, then paint over the area you want to adjust.

Adobe provides four main Brush controls:

  • Size – controls the diameter of the brush
  • Feather – controls how soft the edge of the brush is
  • Flow – controls how quickly the adjustment builds as you paint
  • Density – limits the maximum strength of the brush stroke

For beginners, a reasonably soft feather usually helps the adjustment blend naturally into the surrounding photograph.

Add and Subtract

Automatic masks are remarkably good, but they are not always perfect.

If Select Subject missed part of the subject, choose Add → Brush and paint that area into the mask.

If Lightroom selected something that you do not want included, choose Subtract → Brush and remove it.

This is one of the most useful concepts to understand. You do not have to accept Lightroom’s first selection.

Use the Overlay

When creating or refining a mask, keep an eye on the coloured overlay. It shows the area currently included in the mask.

If the red overlay covers your bird but also covers part of the background, you know the mask needs refinement.

Lightroom Classic also provides different overlay display modes, including colour overlays and black-and-white views, which can make complicated masks easier to inspect. For a beginner, the standard coloured overlay is usually enough.

Linear Gradient

A Linear Gradient is useful when you want an adjustment to fade gradually across part of the photograph.

A classic example is a bright sky. Rather than applying a hard adjustment to the entire sky, drag a Linear Gradient down from the top of the image so the adjustment gradually fades out rather than stopping abruptly.

It can also work well for bright foregrounds, dark edges, light entering from one side, or gradually darkening an unimportant part of the frame.

Radial Gradient

The Radial Gradient creates an oval or circular selection and can be useful when you want to gently draw attention toward a subject.

For example, place a large radial mask around a person’s face or a bird, then make a very small Exposure increase. Alternatively, invert the mask and slightly darken the surrounding area.

The key word here is slightly. If the viewer can clearly see the oval-shaped edit, it is too strong.

A Simple Beginner Example

Imagine a bird sitting against a dark background. The photograph is reasonably exposed, but the bird needs a little more presence.

  1. Choose Masking
  2. Select Subject
  3. Check the red overlay
  4. Add or subtract areas if necessary
  5. Increase Exposure slightly
  6. Open the Shadows a little
  7. Add a small amount of Texture if needed
  8. Turn the mask visibility off temporarily and compare

You may find that a very small adjustment is enough. The objective is not to make the bird obviously brighter. It is simply to guide the viewer’s eye toward it.

Don’t Create the “Cut-Out” Look

One of the most common problems with local adjustments is making the subject look as though it has been pasted onto the photograph.

This happens when the subject becomes much brighter than the background, much sharper than everything else, excessively saturated, or surrounded by an obvious halo.

If this happens, reduce the adjustment. The subject still needs to belong naturally within the light of the original photograph.

Masks Are Non-Destructive

Like Lightroom Classic’s other Develop adjustments, masking is non-destructive. Your original photograph is not permanently altered by these edits.

That means you can experiment. If a mask does not work, change the settings, refine the selection, hide it temporarily, delete it or start again.

A Simple Beginner Workflow

  1. Ask what part of the photograph actually needs changing?
  2. Open Masking
  3. Try Select Subject, Sky or Background first
  4. Check the overlay
  5. Use Add or Subtract if necessary
  6. Make a small adjustment
  7. Turn the mask on and off to compare
  8. Reduce the adjustment if it looks obvious
Final Check
  • Is the mask selecting the correct area?
  • Does the adjustment help the main subject?
  • Does the photograph still look natural?
  • Are there halos around the edge of the mask?
  • Has the subject become unnaturally bright?
  • Would the photograph still work with a slightly weaker adjustment?

If you are unsure, reduce the strength.

Local adjustments should guide the viewer, not announce themselves.

📸 Photographer’s Tips

🌱 Beginner Tip – B010

Know When to Raise Your ISO

A low ISO can give excellent image quality, but keeping ISO too low can sometimes result in a blurred photograph.

If the light is fading or your subject is moving, don’t be afraid to increase the ISO so your camera can use a faster shutter speed.

Modern cameras can produce very good results at higher ISO settings, and some noise can usually be reduced later. Motion blur from an accidentally slow shutter speed is much harder to fix.

The important lesson is simple: don’t sacrifice a sharp photograph just to keep the ISO at 100.

📷 Intermediate Tip – I010

Try Backlighting Your Subject

We often position ourselves so the light falls onto the front of our subject. Sometimes, however, the more interesting photograph is found by shooting towards the light.

With the light behind your subject, you can create glowing edges around hair, feathers, leaves or grasses. Translucent subjects can become particularly striking when illuminated from behind.

Watch your exposure carefully and change your position to control bright highlights and flare.

Next time you’re photographing near sunrise or sunset, walk around your subject and see what happens when the light comes from behind rather than in front.

⭐ Advanced Tip – A010

Pay Attention to the Space Between Things

Composition isn’t only about the objects within the frame. The spaces between them matter too.

Look at the gaps between people, trees, buildings or other important elements. If those gaps become too small, objects can appear to merge or create awkward visual tangents.

Moving the camera only a few centimetres can change these relationships completely.

Before pressing the shutter, stop looking at the subjects themselves for a moment and examine the spaces between them. Clean separation can turn a cluttered composition into one that feels deliberate and resolved.

📷 This Week’s Camera Challenge

Make the Shadow the Subject

This week, forget about photographing the object itself and concentrate on the shadow it creates.

Look for strong directional light from the sun, a window, streetlight or other light source, then build your composition around the shape, pattern or texture of the shadow.

Try photographing:

  • A person without showing the person
  • Railings or fences casting repeating patterns
  • Trees or leaves creating shapes on a wall
  • Everyday objects transformed by their shadows
  • Long shadows near sunrise or sunset

You can include part of the object creating the shadow, but the shadow should be the main reason for taking the photograph.

The challenge is to stop looking only at what the light illuminates and start noticing what the light leaves behind.

Bonus challenge: Make one photograph where someone looking at it cannot immediately tell what created the shadow.
