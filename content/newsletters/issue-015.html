📷 Convention Corner

This week in our Long Exposure series, Ian van der Wolde's practical advice turns to the small, easily overlooked problems that can ruin an exposure lasting several minutes.

Mastering Long Exposure Photography – Part 9: Light Leaks, Vibration and the Problems You Don't See Coming

You've found the composition.

The tripod is solid. Focus is spot on. You've checked the histogram, fitted the ND filter and calculated a five-minute exposure.

You press the shutter.

Now you wait.

Five minutes later the exposure finishes, perhaps followed by another five minutes of long exposure noise reduction.

Then you discover a strange streak across the image, an unexplained patch of light, or that the entire photograph is slightly soft.

During his VAPS Convention presentation, Ian van der Wolde highlighted several seemingly insignificant things that can ruin a long exposure.

Most aren't difficult to prevent.

The trick is knowing to look for them.

The Problem With Long Exposures

In an ordinary photograph taken at 1/250 second, a tiny amount of stray light or a momentary vibration may have virtually no effect.

Leave the shutter open for several minutes and the situation changes.

Small problems have time to accumulate.

Ian gave a useful example from his own photography. He has made exposures of around 16 minutes where distant streetlights, well away from the subject, gradually illuminated the scene.

If useful light can accumulate over 16 minutes, unwanted light can too.

That's why long exposure photography requires us to think about light entering the camera from places other than through the front of the lens.

DSLR Users: Cover the Viewfinder

This was one of Ian's strongest practical tips.

With a DSLR, light can enter through the optical viewfinder.

Normally your eye covers the viewfinder while you're taking a photograph, and with short exposures it may not cause an obvious problem.

But put the camera on a tripod, step away and leave the shutter open for several minutes and light entering through the viewfinder can potentially affect the exposure.

Some DSLRs have a built-in viewfinder shutter. Others were supplied with a small cover designed specifically for this purpose.

If yours doesn't have one, Ian's solution was wonderfully uncomplicated.

Cover it.

A hat, beanie or another suitable light-blocking material can do the job.

The important thing is preventing stray light from entering.

What About Mirrorless Cameras?

This particular problem generally doesn't apply in the same way to mirrorless cameras because they don't have the DSLR's optical path through the viewfinder.

But that doesn't mean mirrorless photographers can ignore light leaks completely.

Your filter system can still cause problems.

Watch the Back of Your ND Filter

This is particularly important when using square or rectangular filters in a holder.

A strong ND filter may be blocking almost all the light coming directly through the lens.

But if there's a gap between the filter and holder, light can enter from behind or around the filter.

During a short exposure, you may never notice it.

During several minutes, that stray light can build up and create unwanted flare, streaks, colour shifts or fogging.

Ian showed filter systems incorporating light baffles or seals designed to prevent this.

He also admitted to once removing a light-blocking component because he didn't think he'd need it.

Experience subsequently proved otherwise!

The lesson is simple:

If your filter system has foam seals, gaskets or light-blocking components, they're there for a reason.

Check for Light Before Starting

Once you've fitted the filter, look carefully around the holder.

Is the filter seated properly?

Can you see a gap?

Is strong sunlight hitting the side or back of the filter?

Is another filter creating a space through which light could enter?

If necessary, shade the filter system from direct light.

Taking thirty seconds to check can save wasting a ten-minute exposure.

Then There's Vibration

Stray light isn't the only invisible enemy.

Movement can be even more frustrating.

Remember that we're asking the camera to remain completely stationary for the entire exposure.

Not almost stationary.

Stationary.

A vibration so small that you don't notice it standing beside the camera may still reduce fine detail in the finished photograph.

Ian's Little Piece of Velcro

One of the simplest tips from Ian's presentation involved something that probably costs only a few cents.

A piece of Velcro.

When Ian uses a wired remote release, he secures the cable to the tripod.

Why?

Imagine photographing beside the coast on a windy evening.

The remote cable is hanging freely from the camera.

The wind catches it.

Tap... tap... tap...

The cable repeatedly hits the tripod leg.

You may barely hear it.

But over a four-minute exposure, those tiny impacts can introduce vibration into the camera.

Ian simply secures the cable so it can't move.

A rubber band or another suitable strap can achieve the same result.

Sometimes the cheapest item in the camera bag can save the photograph.

Watch Where You're Standing

The tripod may be perfectly stable while the ground underneath it isn't.

Timber boardwalks are a classic example.

Someone walks past and the entire structure moves slightly.

Bridges, jetties and viewing platforms can do the same thing.

Even soft sand can shift as water moves around the tripod legs.

Before starting a long exposure, think about what's supporting you and the tripod.

If someone walks across a timber platform halfway through your five-minute exposure, you may have little choice but to start again.

Wind Changes Everything

A lightweight tripod is wonderful when you're carrying it.

A strong coastal wind may make you wish it weighed considerably more.

Keep the tripod as compact as practical.

If possible:

  • Keep the centre column down.
  • Avoid unnecessarily extending the thinnest leg sections.
  • Make sure every leg is firmly planted.
  • Keep loose straps and cables secured.
  • Avoid leaving the camera strap flapping in the wind.

Even the camera strap itself can become a source of vibration.

If conditions are particularly windy, sometimes the best decision is simply to wait.

Remember Ian's recurring message throughout the presentation:

Photography rewards patience.

What About Image Stabilisation?

As we discussed in Part 6, Ian recommended turning image stabilisation off when the camera is mounted securely on a tripod.

However, modern stabilisation systems differ between cameras and lenses. Some automatically detect tripod use, and manufacturer recommendations aren't identical across every system.

So check the instructions for your equipment rather than assuming one rule applies universally.

What matters is eliminating anything that could introduce movement during the exposure.

Don't Touch the Camera

Once everything is ready, avoid physically pressing the shutter button if possible.

A remote release is ideal.

Many cameras can also be triggered using:

  • a self-timer
  • a wireless remote
  • a smartphone app
  • an interval timer

The objective is simply to open the shutter without disturbing the camera.

For very long exposures using Bulb mode, Ian described using a locking cable release and his phone as the timer.

Simple, reliable and effective.

Ian's Boat Trick

Ian also shared a clever example of just how flexible long exposure photography can be.

He was making a long exposure when boats kept travelling through the scene with their lights on.

Those lights would have created bright streaks across the photograph.

Instead of abandoning the exposure, Ian simply covered the front of the lens while each boat passed.

He counted how long the lens had been covered, then added that time to the end of the exposure.

The shutter remained open, but while the lens was covered no meaningful light reached the sensor.

Once the boat had passed, he uncovered the lens and continued.

It is a wonderful example of the deliberate, problem-solving approach Ian brings to long exposure photography.

When you're working in minutes rather than fractions of a second, you sometimes have time to solve problems while the photograph is being made.

Before You Press the Shutter

Here's a useful checklist based on Ian's advice:

✓ Tripod stable

✓ Centre column down where practical

✓ Focus checked

✓ Image stabilisation set appropriately

✓ ND filter seated correctly

✓ Filter holder checked for light gaps

✓ DSLR viewfinder covered

✓ Remote cable secured

✓ Camera strap secured

✓ Nothing loose that can catch the wind

✓ Tripod standing on stable ground

✓ Timer ready

Then open the shutter.

Once the exposure starts, leave the camera alone.

📷 Try It This Week

This week's exercise is slightly different.

Before making your next long exposure, spend one minute inspecting the camera rather than looking through it.

Walk around the tripod.

Look at everything that could potentially move.

Check the camera strap.

Check the remote cable.

Check the tripod legs.

Check the filter holder.

If you're using a DSLR, cover the viewfinder.

Then make your exposure.

The objective isn't to create a particular photograph this week.

It's to develop a pre-exposure routine.

Once these little checks become habits, you won't need to consciously think about them.

And that means you can spend more time thinking about the photograph.

💡 Quick Tip

Long exposures magnify small mistakes.

A tiny light leak, a cable tapping against the tripod or movement you would never notice at 1/250 second can become visible when the shutter remains open for several minutes.

Before starting a long exposure, take a few seconds to ask:

What can move, and where can unwanted light get in?

Fix those problems first.

Then press the shutter.

💻 Editing Corner

Presets can be one of the fastest ways to speed up your Lightroom workflow, but they are also easy to misuse. The key is to treat a preset as a starting point rather than the finished edit.

Lightroom Basics – Part 8: Using Presets Properly

Presets can be one of the fastest ways to speed up your Lightroom workflow, but they are also easy to misuse.

A preset is simply a saved collection of editing settings. When you apply one, Lightroom changes the adjustments included in that preset. Adobe describes presets as reusable settings for adjustments such as exposure, contrast and colour grading. Adobe’s guide to presets

The important thing to understand is this:

A preset should usually be a starting point, not the finished edit.

1. What a Preset Actually Does

A preset may change settings such as:

  • Exposure
  • Contrast
  • Highlights and Shadows
  • White Balance
  • Texture and Clarity
  • Colour
  • Tone Curve
  • HSL
  • Sharpening
  • Vignetting

Some presets make only a few subtle changes.

Others may dramatically alter the entire look of a photograph.

That is why two different photographs can respond very differently to the same preset.

2. Apply the Preset, Then Look at the Photograph

In Lightroom Classic, presets are available in the Presets panel in the Develop module.

Choose a preset and then look at what it has actually done.

Do not assume that because the preset looked good on one photograph, it must also suit another.

Ask:

  • Is the photograph now too dark?
  • Are the highlights too bright?
  • Has the colour become unnatural?
  • Is the subject still the main focus?
  • Has the preset changed the mood in a useful way?

If you spot a problem or the effect does not suit the photograph, adjust the settings or choose another preset.

3. Use Presets for Consistency

One of the strongest reasons to use presets is consistency.

Imagine photographing:

  • A wedding
  • A club event
  • A sports competition
  • A series of landscapes
  • A travel collection

You may want the photographs to have a similar overall look.

A preset can provide that starting point quickly.

You can then fine-tune each photograph individually.

This is much more reliable than trying to make every photograph identical with fixed slider values.

4. Adjust After Applying

After applying a preset, work through the photograph normally.

Check:

  • Exposure
  • White Balance
  • Highlights
  • Shadows
  • Whites
  • Blacks
  • Colour
  • Local adjustments

The preset may have given you the overall style, but the photograph still needs to be treated as an individual image.

Beginner rule

Never be afraid to change a preset.

You are not “breaking” it.

You are simply finishing the edit.

5. Watch White Balance Carefully

Presets that include White Balance settings can create problems when photographs were taken under different lighting conditions.

For example:

A preset created for golden-hour light may make an indoor photograph far too warm.

A preset created for cool landscape light may make a portrait look unnaturally blue.

If the colour feels wrong, check Temp and Tint before changing everything else.

This is where Part 4 of our Lightroom Basics series becomes useful.

6. Watch Exposure Too

Exposure can also vary enormously between photographs.

A preset may work beautifully on a well-exposed image but make an underexposed photograph too dark.

That does not mean the preset is bad.

It simply means the starting photograph is different.

Adjust the Exposure slider until the subject looks right.

Presets Are Not Filters

This is a useful distinction.

A preset is not simply a decorative effect placed over the photograph.

It is a collection of Lightroom editing instructions.

Because those settings remain editable, you can continue adjusting the photograph after the preset has been applied.

This is one of the advantages of working with presets inside Lightroom.

7. Create Your Own Preset

Once you develop an editing style that you use repeatedly, you can create your own preset.

In Lightroom Classic:

  1. Edit a photograph the way you like.
  2. Open the Presets panel in the Develop module.
  3. Click the + button and choose Create Preset.
  4. Select the settings you want included.
  5. Give the preset a meaningful name and choose its group.
  6. Click Create.

Adobe allows you to choose which Develop settings are included when creating a custom preset. Adobe’s preset-creation instructions

This is useful because you may not want every setting saved.

For example, you might save:

  • Contrast
  • Tone Curve
  • Colour adjustments

but leave out:

  • Exposure
  • White Balance
  • Local masks

Those settings often need to vary from photograph to photograph.

A Better Way to Build Your Own Preset

If you are creating a general-purpose preset, consider excluding adjustments that are highly photograph-specific.

A useful preset might include:

  • Tone Curve
  • HSL adjustments
  • Colour Grading
  • Texture
  • Clarity
  • Sharpening
  • Calibration

But leave out settings such as:

  • Exposure
  • White Balance
  • Subject-specific masks

Make your crop and any spot removal individually as part of finishing each photograph.

This makes the preset much more flexible.

8. Use Preset Amount Carefully

Lightroom Classic allows the strength of supported presets to be adjusted with the Preset Amount slider.

This means you can apply a preset and then reduce or increase its effect instead of manually changing every individual slider.

If the preset looks too strong, try reducing the Amount.

Sometimes 70% of a preset looks better than 100%.

If the Amount slider is greyed out, that preset may not have Support Amount Slider enabled, or it may contain settings that do not support this control. When creating your own preset, enable Support Amount Slider if it is available and you want this flexibility. Adobe’s guidance on the Amount slider

Don't Chase Someone Else's Look

There are thousands of commercial presets available online.

Some are excellent.

Some are little more than aggressively edited slider settings.

The most important question is not whether a preset is popular.

It is whether it improves your photograph.

A preset should support the photograph rather than force every photograph into the same style.

A Simple Beginner Workflow

When using a preset:

  1. Edit or assess the basic exposure first.
  2. Apply the preset.
  3. Look at the whole photograph.
  4. Adjust Exposure.
  5. Check White Balance.
  6. Check Highlights and Shadows.
  7. Reduce the Preset Amount if necessary and available.
  8. Make any local adjustments.
  9. Compare before and after.
Final Check

Ask yourself:

  • Does the preset suit this photograph?
  • Does the colour still look believable?
  • Is the exposure correct?
  • Has the photograph become too contrasty?
  • Does the image still look natural?
  • Have I adjusted the preset to suit this photograph?

If the photograph looks as though it has been “preset”, rather than edited, reduce the effect.

Remember

Presets save time. They do not replace judgement.

Use them to establish a look quickly, then finish the photograph yourself.

📸 Photographer's Tips

🌱 Beginner Tip – B011

Focus on the Eyes

When photographing people or animals, the eyes are usually the most important part of the image to have sharply focused.

If your camera has eye-detection autofocus, learn how to activate it and check that it is locking onto the correct eye. If you're selecting the focus point yourself, place it directly over the eye rather than relying on the camera to decide.

When the subject is turned slightly away from you, concentrate on the eye closest to the camera.

A photograph can tolerate many things being slightly soft, but an unintentionally soft eye is very noticeable.

📷 Intermediate Tip – I011

Use Shutter Speed Creatively

Shutter speed isn't only about making photographs sharp. It can also determine how movement looks in your image.

A fast shutter speed can freeze splashing water, a bird in flight or a sportsperson in action. A slower shutter speed can deliberately show movement in waterfalls, waves, traffic or people.

Instead of automatically choosing a shutter speed that freezes everything, ask:

Do I want to freeze the movement or show it?

Make that decision first, then choose the shutter speed that creates the effect you want.

⭐ Advanced Tip – A011

Use Colour as Part of the Composition

Colour isn't simply something your subject happens to have. It can become one of the structural elements of your photograph.

Look for relationships between colours. Complementary colours such as blue and orange can create strong contrast, while similar colours can produce a quieter, more harmonious image.

Also watch for a small area of intense colour within an otherwise subdued scene. It can attract attention just as strongly as a bright highlight.

Before taking the photograph, consider where the colour is directing the viewer's eye and whether that's where you want them to look.

📷 This Week's Camera Challenge

Shutter Speed: Freeze It or Show It

This week, photograph movement in two deliberately different ways.

First, choose a shutter speed fast enough to freeze the action. Then photograph movement again using a slower shutter speed so that some motion becomes part of the photograph.

The Warrnambool Running Festival this weekend provides a perfect opportunity to try both approaches, but you don't need to photograph runners. Cars, cyclists, birds, pets, waves or even people walking can work.

If you're photographing the Running Festival: concentrate first on getting your safe, sharp photographs. Once you have those, you might experiment with a slower shutter speed and panning.

Then compare the results.

Which photograph tells the story of movement better?

The aim isn't to decide that fast or slow is better. It is to learn that shutter speed is a creative decision, not simply a setting used to prevent camera shake.
