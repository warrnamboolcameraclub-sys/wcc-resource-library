⚫ Black & White Photography Workshop

A big thank you to Peter for presenting Saturday's Black & White Photography Workshop.

Although the weather forced us to change our original plans, it certainly didn't dampen the enthusiasm of those who attended. Peter shared his approach to black and white photography, encouraging us to look beyond colour and instead concentrate on light, shadow, texture, shape and composition.

One message came through particularly strongly. Great photographs don't have to be taken in spectacular locations. Beauty can often be found in the ordinary places we walk past every day, provided we take the time to really look.

The relaxed format encouraged plenty of discussion and questions, making it an informative and enjoyable afternoon for everyone.

After the workshop, most of the group continued the conversations over dinner at Bojangles, where Peter and his wife Dianne joined us. It was a wonderful way to finish the day, with plenty of photography stories, laughter and good company around the table.

Thank you again, Peter, for spending the day with us.

✂️ This Wednesday – First Mat Cutting Session

One of the initiatives we've been talking about for some time is finally becoming a reality.

Our first Mat Cutting Session will be held this Wednesday at 7:00 pm.

Preparing prints for competition can seem daunting if you don't have access to a quality mat cutter or haven't mounted a print before. These evenings are designed to remove that barrier by giving members access to the equipment, practical guidance and support needed to prepare prints with confidence.

Members will be able to:

  • Measure and plan mat borders.
  • Cut a mat board to size.
  • Prepare boards for the window opening.

  • Receive practical assistance throughout the process.

The large work table provides plenty of room for members to prepare and trim their mats before moving to the cutter.

If you would like to attend, please email or text me directly and I'll send you my address.

📷 Convention Corner

Mastering Long Exposure Photography

Finding Beauty Close to Home

“You don’t need to travel to Iceland to create beautiful landscape photographs. Some of my favourite images were taken just minutes from home.”
Ian van der Wolde

One of the most refreshing messages from Ian van der Wolde’s VAPS Convention presentation was not about shutter speeds, filters or expensive cameras.

It was about changing the way we see the places around us.

As photographers, it is easy to believe that great images only exist in spectacular locations. Social media constantly presents us with photographs of Icelandic waterfalls, Norwegian fjords, African wildlife and the Northern Lights.

Those destinations are undoubtedly beautiful, but Ian reminded us that compelling photographs can often be found much closer to home.

Many of his award-winning images were created at familiar Victorian locations, including Brighton Baths, Black Rock, St Kilda Pier, Clifton Springs, Coronella, Kitty Miller Bay and numerous small coastal locations that thousands of people walk past every week.

The difference was not the location.

The difference was the photographer.

Learning to See

One of Ian’s favourite examples came from Clifton Springs.

While photographing with another photographer, his companion immediately headed down to the water’s edge to capture the obvious scene.

Ian paused in the car park, looked down from the higher vantage point and recognised a completely different composition.

The resulting image became part of the portfolio that later won Victorian Landscape Photographer of the Year.

The lesson is simple.

Before rushing towards the obvious subject:

  • Stop and look around
  • Look behind you
  • Look higher
  • Look lower
  • Walk another twenty metres
  • Consider where everyone else is standing, then try somewhere different

Sometimes the strongest photograph is not found at the most obvious viewpoint.

Photograph the Ordinary

Throughout his presentation, Ian showed images of:

  • A stormwater outlet
  • A bathing box
  • A local pier
  • A breakwater
  • An old fence
  • A section of bridge architecture
  • A simple line of posts disappearing into the water

None of these subjects is especially remarkable on its own.

What made the images remarkable was thoughtful composition, careful use of light and the deliberate use of long exposure to simplify the scene.

Interesting photographs rarely begin with interesting subjects.

They begin with interesting ways of seeing.

Become Familiar with Your Local Area

There is a tremendous advantage in photographing close to home.

You can return.

If the tide is wrong today, come back tomorrow.

If the sky is clear, return when a weather front approaches.

If the light is harsh, revisit the location at sunrise, sunset or during blue hour.

Professional landscape photographers often revisit the same location many times before producing the image they envisioned.

Unlike a holiday destination where you may have only one opportunity, local locations allow you to wait for the right conditions rather than simply accepting the conditions you have.

Slow Down

Ian spoke about often spending half an hour producing a single photograph.

During that time, he is not simply waiting for the shutter to close.

He is observing.

  • Watching the clouds.
  • Studying the changing light.
  • Thinking about where he might position the camera next.
  • Planning another composition.

This slower and more deliberate approach often leads to stronger photographs because it encourages us to become immersed in the scene rather than simply recording it.

Build Your Own Location Library

One of Ian’s practical suggestions was to use your smartphone to record potential locations.

When you notice an interesting composition while driving or walking:

  • Take a quick reference photograph
  • Record the location
  • Note the direction of the light
  • Consider what lens might suit the scene
  • Return when the conditions are more favourable

Over time, you can build a personal catalogue of photographic locations ready for those mornings or evenings when the light looks promising.

Many photographers spend more time deciding where to go than they do taking photographs.

A location library helps solve that problem.

The Camera Is Not the Most Important Part

One comment from Ian summed up the presentation perfectly:

“It’s not the camera that takes the photograph.”

Equipment certainly has its place, but creativity begins with observation.

A photographer who learns to recognise composition, balance, light and atmosphere will consistently produce stronger photographs than someone relying solely on expensive equipment.

📸 This Week’s Camera Challenge

Choose a location within ten kilometres of your home.

Visit it without expecting to create a masterpiece.

Instead, spend twenty minutes simply observing.

Challenge yourself to identify:

  • Five interesting compositions
  • Three different camera heights
  • Two unusual viewpoints
  • One photograph that most people would walk straight past

Take your time.

You may be surprised by how many photographic opportunities exist in places you have driven past hundreds of times.

📸 Photographer's Tips

🌱 Beginner Tip – B002

Learn Exposure Compensation

One of the quickest ways to improve your photography is to become familiar with your camera's Exposure Compensation (+/-) control.

When shooting in Aperture Priority or Shutter Priority, Exposure Compensation lets you make an image brighter or darker without switching to Manual mode.

Try +1 when photographing bright subjects like white birds or sandy beaches, and -1 for very dark subjects. It's a simple adjustment that can dramatically improve your results.

📷 Intermediate Tip – I002

Change Your Focal Length Before Your Position

When a composition isn't working, many photographers instinctively zoom.

Instead, try moving your feet first.

Changing your camera position often has a much bigger impact on perspective, foreground relationships and background distractions than simply changing focal length. Once you've found the best viewpoint, then choose the lens that suits the composition.

⭐ Advanced Tip – A002

Read the Light Before Raising the Camera

Experienced photographers often spend more time looking than shooting.

Before setting up your tripod or raising your camera, take a moment to study the direction of the light, the quality of the shadows and how your subject is illuminated.

The light will usually tell you where the best photograph is waiting to be made.
