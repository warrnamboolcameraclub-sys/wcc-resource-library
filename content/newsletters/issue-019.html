📷 Open the Werribee Photography Preparation Guide

What Should You Bring?

Tania's suggested camera kit is nice and simple:

  • Camera body and spare battery
  • Extra memory cards
  • Your favourite lens
  • Comfortable camera bag
  • Tripod or monopod if you like using one

Also remember comfortable walking shoes, water, sunscreen and a hat. You can buy food at the zoo or bring your own food and snacks. The zoo is cashless, so make sure you have a card or suitable digital payment option with you.

Which Lens?

There is no single perfect zoo lens. A telephoto zoom is probably the most useful option for animal portraits and subjects that are some distance away. Something in the 70–200 mm, 100–400 mm or 200–500 mm range can work very well.

But don't automatically leave the shorter lens at home. A standard zoom can be useful for animals within the landscape, wider environmental photographs, groups and photographs of the Club outing itself.

Sometimes the strongest zoo photograph isn't the tightest one.

Suggested Starting Camera Settings

Wildlife can go from completely stationary to moving very quickly, so settings that allow you to react are useful.

🚌 Shooting From the Safari Bus

Remember that even when the animal is standing still, you may not be. The vehicle can introduce vibration and movement.

  • Use a fast enough shutter speed.
  • Keep image stabilisation on.
  • Use short bursts rather than holding the shutter down.
  • Track the animal before taking the photograph.
  • Avoid pressing the lens firmly against the vehicle, where vibration may transfer directly to the camera.

And occasionally put the long lens down. A giraffe, rhino or antelope within the open-range environment can make a much more interesting photograph than another tightly cropped animal portrait.

🪟 Shooting Through Glass

Glass introduces two main problems: reflections and loss of contrast.

  • Get the front of the lens as close to the glass as practical.
  • Use the lens hood to help block stray light.
  • Photograph as square to the glass as possible.
  • Shade the lens from light behind you.
  • Turn the flash off.
  • Bright clothing can reflect in the glass, so darker clothing may help.

A polarising filter may reduce some reflections, but it also reduces the amount of light reaching the camera and will not eliminate every reflection.

And, of course, never tap or bang on the glass to try to get an animal's attention.

🕸 Shooting Through Wire or Mesh

Wire fences don't necessarily mean the photograph is lost.

  • Get the lens close to the fence where it is safe and practical.
  • Use a longer focal length.
  • Use a relatively wide aperture.
  • Focus on the animal, not the wire.
  • Try to keep plenty of distance between the fence and the animal.

If autofocus keeps grabbing the wire, switch to a smaller AF area or single focus point. A small move left or right can also place the lens through a better opening in the mesh.

🌿 Avoid the “Zoo Look”

One of the most enjoyable challenges is making a photograph that doesn't immediately look as though it was taken at a zoo.

Before pressing the shutter, check the background for fences, gates, signs, buildings, feeding equipment, concrete edges, visitors, rubbish bins, rails and posts.

Often you only need to move slightly left or right, get lower, wait for the animal to change position or use a longer focal length and wider aperture.

👀 Watch Before You Shoot

Don't spend the entire day looking through the viewfinder. Watch the animals.

Look for repeated behaviour such as walking the same path, interacting with another animal, grooming, feeding, play, eye contact or an interesting expression. Once you recognise a pattern, you can often prepare for the photograph before the moment happens.

🎯 Werribee Challenge: Try to come home with three different types of photographs: a strong animal portrait, a photograph showing behaviour, and an environmental image showing the animal within its surroundings.

Bonus challenge: make at least one photograph where somebody looking at it would not immediately know it was taken at a zoo.

📸 Convention Corner

Protecting Your Photos – Part 1

Don't Trust a Simple Copy

At the VAPS Convention, photographer and storage specialist Cedric Robertson posed a simple question:

Does your priceless photo collection have a single point of failure?

Over the coming weeks, we'll explore some of the practical advice from Cedric's presentation and look at simple ways we can make our own photography workflows safer.

We'll start right at the beginning: getting the photographs off the memory card.

Open Part 1 – Don't Trust a Simple Copy

Copying isn't necessarily verifying

Most of us have done it. We come home after a shoot, insert the memory card, drag a folder of photographs onto the computer or an external drive and wait for the copy to finish.

The files are there, so job done.

Cedric's advice was to go one step further.

For important photographs, consider using software capable of verifying the copy. Verification can use a checksum or hash to compare the source data with the copied data and confirm that what was written matches what was read from the card.

That is different from simply seeing that the files have appeared in the destination folder.

Make another copy

Cedric also stressed the importance of avoiding a single point of failure.

If you copy your photographs from the memory card onto one hard drive and then format the card, you are left with just one copy of your photographs.

If that drive fails, so does your only copy.

Lightroom Classic users can use Make a Second Copy To during import to create an additional copy in another location. Other applications and dedicated ingest software can provide similar functionality, with some offering more extensive verification options.

The important principle is not which program you use. It is that you develop a repeatable workflow that doesn't leave your photographs sitting in only one place.

Don't rush to format the card

There is also no prize for being the fastest person to format a memory card.

Before reusing it, make sure your photographs have transferred successfully and that you have another safe copy.

For particularly important shoots, you may choose to keep the card untouched until you know your backup routine has completed successfully.

This week's practical tip

After your next shoot, look at exactly what happens between pressing the shutter and formatting the memory card.

How many copies of those photographs exist?

If the answer is only one, you've found the first weakness in your backup workflow.

💻 Editing Corner

Lightroom Basics – Part 12

Putting It All Together: A Competition Workflow

Over the past eleven parts of Lightroom Basics, we have looked at individual editing tools and techniques. This week we bring them together into a simple workflow for preparing an image for a Warrnambool Camera Club competition.

The aim is not to use every Lightroom tool on every photograph. It is to work through the image in a logical order, make deliberate decisions and finish with a competition file that looks the way you intended.

Open Part 12 – The Complete Competition Workflow

1. Start With the Photograph, Not the Sliders

Before editing, ask yourself why you selected this image. Is it the subject, light, moment, colour, composition, story, expression or atmosphere?

Try to identify the strongest part of the photograph before you begin. The purpose of editing is to strengthen the photograph you already have, not simply to move sliders until something looks different.

2. Check the Crop and Composition

Look at the overall composition. Check the subject position, unnecessary space, horizon, distracting edge elements and whether a different crop would make the subject stronger.

3. Get the Overall Exposure Right

Use the Histogram as a guide and check Exposure, Highlights, Shadows, Whites and Blacks. Establish a sound tonal foundation before making detailed adjustments.

4. Check White Balance and Colour

Look at Temperature, Tint, overall colour balance, excessive saturation and unwanted colour casts. A warmer or cooler image should support the mood rather than simply make the image more dramatic.

5. Refine Contrast

Use small adjustments to Contrast, Highlights, Shadows, Whites, Blacks or the Tone Curve. Ask whether the adjustment is improving separation, depth and attention within the photograph.

6. Remove Distractions

Look carefully around the frame for dust spots, sensor marks, bright distractions, small unwanted objects and objects entering from the edge.

7. Make Local Adjustments

Only after the overall photograph is working should you consider masks and local adjustments. Brighten the subject, darken an overly bright background or refine a distracting area, but keep the adjustment subtle.

8. Check Sharpening and Noise

Zoom to around 100% and inspect important detail. Apply enough sharpening to support the photograph without harsh edges or halos, and use noise reduction only as much as needed.

9. Step Away From It

Leave the image for a few minutes, an hour or even overnight if you can. A fresh look often reveals colour, contrast, crop or local adjustments that have been pushed too far.

10. Compare Before and After

Use Lightroom's Before/After view and ask: Did I actually improve the photograph?

11. Prepare the Competition File

The final digital competition file should be:

JPEG • sRGB • fit within 1920 × 1080 pixels • maximum 5 MB

These limits apply to EDPI entries and the digital copy submitted with print entries.

Image ShapePossible Export
16:9 landscape1920 × 1080
3:2 landscape1620 × 1080

Do not stretch or distort an image to make it exactly 1920 × 1080. If you saved the preset from Part 11, use WCC Competition — 1920x1080 — 5MB.

12. Look at the Exported File

Open the actual JPEG you are going to submit and check the crop, sharpness, colour, dust spots, dimensions, file size and filename.

A Simple Competition Workflow

Select → Crop → Exposure → Colour → Contrast → Clean Up → Local Adjustments → Sharpen/Noise → Review → Export → Check

Final question: What is the first thing I want the judge to notice?

If something else attracts your attention first, consider whether the crop, brightness, colour or local adjustments are directing the viewer to the wrong place.

There is no Lightroom slider labelled “Make This a Better Photograph.” Good competition preparation comes from lots of small decisions made with a purpose.

📸 Photographer's Tips – Set 15

🌱 Beginner Tip – B015

RAW vs JPEG: What's the Difference?

Your camera can usually save photographs as JPEG, RAW or both, but what's the difference?

A JPEG is processed by your camera and is ready to share or print. It takes up less storage space but offers less flexibility when making significant adjustments later.

A RAW file retains more of the information captured by your camera's sensor, giving you greater flexibility to adjust exposure, white balance and other settings during editing.

RAW files are generally larger and require processing before they're ready to share.

If you're unsure which to use, try setting your camera to RAW + JPEG. You'll have a ready-to-use image and a RAW file to experiment with in Lightroom or other editing software.

📷 Intermediate Tip – I015

Understanding Depth of Field

Depth of field determines how much of your photograph appears acceptably sharp in front of and behind the point of focus.

A wide aperture, such as f/2.8, can produce a shallow depth of field, helping your subject stand out against a blurred background.

A smaller aperture, such as f/11, generally increases depth of field, making it useful for landscapes where you want more of the scene to appear sharp.

But aperture isn't the only factor. Focal length, focusing distance and subject-to-background distance also influence the result.

Try photographing the same subject at different apertures and distances. Notice how the background changes, even when your subject remains in focus.

⭐ Advanced Tip – A015

Intentional Camera Movement: Painting With Your Camera

Not every photograph needs to be sharp. Sometimes deliberately moving your camera during an exposure can produce striking abstract images.

This technique, known as Intentional Camera Movement (ICM), uses a relatively slow shutter speed while moving the camera in a controlled direction.

Try moving vertically when photographing trees, horizontally across a coastline, or rotating the camera slightly during the exposure.

Start by experimenting with shutter speeds around 1/4 to 1 second, adjusting according to the light and the effect you're trying to achieve. In bright conditions, an ND filter may be necessary.

The challenge is to create movement that feels deliberate rather than accidental.

Look for subjects with strong colours, shapes or lines, and experiment until the movement becomes part of the composition.

📷 This Week's Camera Challenge

Photograph Through an Obstacle

With our Werribee Open Range Zoo outing coming up, this week's challenge is about learning to photograph through something that would normally get in the way.

That could be glass, wire or mesh, fencing, foliage, branches, a doorway, a window or another foreground object.

The aim is to make the obstruction either disappear from the photograph or become a deliberate part of the composition.

Try getting your lens close to the obstacle, using a wider aperture, choosing a longer focal length or changing your position slightly.

If shooting through glass, watch for reflections and keep the lens as square to the glass as practical. If shooting through mesh or wire, try to place the lens close to the fence while keeping your subject well behind it.

🎯 The Challenge

Create a photograph where you have deliberately shot through an obstacle, but the obstacle does not distract from the final image.

Bonus points if someone looking at the photograph would never know there was glass, wire or fencing between you and the subject.

It is the perfect bit of practice before Werribee.
