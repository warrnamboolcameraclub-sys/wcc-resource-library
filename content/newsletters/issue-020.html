📸 Convention Corner

Protecting Your Photos – Part 2

Does Your Photo Collection Have a Single Point of Failure?

In Part 1 of our series based on Cedric Robertson's presentation at the VAPS Convention, we looked at safely transferring photographs from memory cards and making a second copy before formatting them.

This week, we're looking at the question at the heart of Cedric's presentation:

What happens if the one device holding your photographs suddenly fails?

Open Part 2 – Find the Weakest Link in Your Backup

The $40,000 Photography Trip

During the introduction to Cedric's presentation, an extraordinary example was shared.

A five-week photographic trip to Africa had cost approximately $40,000 and produced around 80,000 photographs.

Imagine returning home with those images, spending months sorting and editing them, and then losing the entire collection because a hard drive failed.

The financial cost of the trip would be substantial, but the photographs themselves might be impossible to replace.

And it doesn't have to be an expensive overseas adventure. Think about photographs of family, weddings, children growing up, or special moments that can never be recreated.

What would it cost you to lose your photographic collection?

What Is a Single Point of Failure?

A single point of failure is anything in your storage system that, if it fails, could result in losing your photographs.

  • One external hard drive: If it fails, your photographs could be lost.
  • One computer: If all your photographs are stored on its internal drive, a drive failure could leave you with nothing.
  • Two drives in the same location: A fire, flood or theft could destroy both copies.
  • One NAS: Even with multiple drives and RAID protection, the entire device could be lost or damaged.

Cedric explained that hard drives do fail. Some failures happen without warning, and recovering the files is not always possible.

Two Copies Are Better, But Location Matters

Let's say you've followed the advice from Part 1 and now have your photographs on your computer and an external backup drive.

That's an important improvement.

But what if both devices are sitting on the same desk?

A house fire, theft or other major incident could still destroy both copies.

Cedric emphasised that keeping copies in different rooms of the same house doesn't provide meaningful protection against a disaster affecting the whole property.

For that, at least one copy needs to be stored somewhere physically separate, such as another property or a suitable cloud backup service.

This Week's Practical Tip: Find Your Weakest Link

Take five minutes to consider where your photographs are stored.

  1. If my computer failed tonight, could I recover every photograph?
  2. If my external hard drive failed, would another copy exist?
  3. If my house suffered a fire or theft, would my photographs survive somewhere else?
  4. Have I actually checked that my backup files can be opened?

If any answer is no, you've identified a potential weakness in your backup system.

You don't necessarily need expensive equipment to fix it. Sometimes an additional external drive, stored securely at another location and updated regularly, can make a significant difference.

📸 Photographer's Tips – Set 16

🌱 Beginner Tip – B016

Understand Your Camera's Metering Modes

Your camera measures the light in a scene to help determine the correct exposure, but did you know it can measure that light in different ways?

Matrix or Evaluative Metering considers the entire scene and is a good choice for most everyday photography.

Centre-Weighted Metering gives greater importance to the middle of the frame, making it useful when your subject is centrally positioned.

Spot Metering measures light from a small area, allowing you to expose for a particular part of your subject.

Try photographing a subject against a bright window using different metering modes. You'll soon discover how the camera's choice of what to measure can dramatically change the exposure.

We'll take a much deeper look at metering modes, when to use them and where they can catch you out in a future edition.

📷 Intermediate Tip – I016

Create Stronger Silhouettes

Silhouettes can turn an ordinary subject into a striking photograph by emphasising its shape rather than its detail.

Position your subject against a brighter background, such as a sunset, bright sky or illuminated window. Expose for the brighter background so that your subject becomes dark.

Look for subjects with recognisable outlines, such as trees, people, birds or architectural features.

Pay particular attention to separation. If two subjects overlap, their shapes may merge into an unrecognisable mass.

A successful silhouette relies on strong shapes, clear outlines and thoughtful composition rather than detail within the subject.

⭐ Advanced Tip – A016

Master Mixed Lighting and Colour Temperature

Photographing a scene illuminated by different light sources can create challenging colour casts.

For example, a room may contain warm tungsten lighting, cooler daylight from a window and LED lights with yet another colour temperature.

Setting a single white balance won't necessarily make every part of the scene look natural.

Experienced photographers can manage this by controlling the light sources, using gels to bring different lights closer together in colour, or selectively adjusting colour during post-processing.

For critical work, remember that different light sources may also have different colour-rendering characteristics, meaning white balance adjustments alone cannot always correct every colour.

The goal isn't necessarily to remove every colour difference, but to make those differences work deliberately within the photograph.

📷 This Week's Camera Challenge

Tell the Story in Three Frames

With Werribee this weekend and the Multicultural Festival not far away, this week's challenge is about thinking beyond the single photograph.

Choose a subject, activity or location and tell its story using three photographs.

1. The Wide Shot

Show where you are and establish the scene.

2. The Main Subject

Move closer and concentrate on the person, animal, action or subject that matters most.

3. The Detail

Look for something small that helps complete the story: hands, eyes, equipment, food, texture, a sign, clothing or another interesting detail.

The three photographs should work together rather than simply being three versions of the same composition.

🎯 The Challenge

When you put the three images together, someone who wasn't there should have a better understanding of the place, event or activity.

This is particularly useful practice for event photography, where the strongest coverage often comes from combining wide views, key moments and small details rather than relying entirely on individual hero shots.
