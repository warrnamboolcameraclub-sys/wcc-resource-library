🏛️ Feature Article

More Than Just a Meeting – Why Our AGM Matters

When most people hear the words Annual General Meeting (AGM), they often think of reports, elections and formalities.

While those things are certainly part of the evening, the AGM is much more than that. It's one of the few occasions each year where every member has an opportunity to help shape the future of our Club.

Read why our AGM matters

The Warrnambool Camera Club belongs to its members. Throughout the year, the Committee manages the day-to-day running of the Club, but it does so on behalf of the membership. The AGM provides an opportunity to reflect on the past year, elect the Committee for the year ahead and discuss the direction of the Club moving forward.

This year's AGM is particularly important as members will be asked to consider the proposed amendments to the Club Rules.

The Committee has recently distributed the latest draft, and we'd encourage every member to take a little time to read through the document before the meeting. While considerable work has gone into reviewing and modernising the Rules, we'd genuinely like to hear your thoughts. If you have suggestions, questions or notice something that could be improved, please let us know before the AGM.

Constructive feedback helps ensure the final document reflects the needs of the membership.

After all, these aren't the Committee's Rules—they're your Club Rules.

The AGM is also a reminder that a successful club relies on more than just a committee. Every workshop, competition, field trip, guest speaker and social activity happens because members volunteer their time and ideas.

Whether you've been a member for many years or have only recently joined, your involvement helps shape the future of our Club.

We hope you'll join us on Thursday, 6 August for the AGM.

Don't forget that competition images are also due that evening, so please ensure your entries are uploaded before the deadline.

Following the AGM, we'll be delighted to welcome Craig Richards for what promises to be one of the highlights of our year. Craig's landscape photography presentation will commence after the AGM, before returning on Saturday, 15 August to conduct his hands-on landscape photography workshop.

We look forward to seeing as many members as possible over what promises to be a fantastic fortnight.

📷 Convention Corner

Mastering Long Exposure Photography

Part 4: Building a Stable Foundation

"The sharpest photographs begin long before you press the shutter."

One of the biggest surprises from Ian van der Wolde's presentation was how little he spoke about cameras.

Instead, he spent considerable time discussing the one piece of equipment that many photographers underestimate – the tripod.

In long exposure photography, every second the shutter remains open is another opportunity for the camera to move. Whether you're photographing for one second or one minute, even the slightest vibration can reduce the sharpness of your image.

For Ian, the tripod isn't an accessory. It's the foundation of every photograph.

Read Part 4: Building a Stable Foundation

Ian's Professional Setup

After almost 40 years as a professional photographer, Ian has settled on equipment he knows he can trust in any conditions.

His current landscape kit includes:

  • Gitzo carbon fibre tripod
  • Arca-Swiss Cube geared head

Together, this combination costs well over $4,000, making it well beyond the budget of many photographers.

So why did Ian choose it?

Quite simply, because it works.

The tripod is exceptionally rigid, lightweight and reliable, while the geared head allows incredibly precise adjustments when fine-tuning a composition.

When photographing seascapes, horizons or architectural lines, being able to move the camera by just a few millimetres can make all the difference.

The Lesson Isn't the Brand

One of the key takeaways from Ian's presentation wasn't that everyone should buy a Gitzo tripod.

It was that your support system should never be the weakest part of your camera kit.

A quality tripod will probably outlast several camera bodies.

Technology changes every few years.

A well-made tripod can easily last twenty years or more.

Ian's advice could be summed up in one sentence:

Buy the best tripod you can comfortably afford.

Great Results Don't Require a $4,000 Tripod

Fortunately, today's photographers have access to some excellent alternatives that offer outstanding performance without the premium price tag.

Budget Recommended Setup Approx. Price
Professional Gitzo Carbon Fibre + Arca-Swiss Cube $4,500+
Advanced Enthusiast Leofoto Ranger Carbon Fibre with Ball Head Around $600
Excellent Value Benro Tortoise Carbon Fibre with Ball Head Around $450
Entry Level Vanguard or K&F Concept Aluminium/Carbon Fibre Tripod Kits $150–350

For many camera club members, a Leofoto or Benro carbon fibre tripod represents an excellent balance between performance, portability and price.

These tripods are stable enough for long exposure photography, light enough to carry comfortably and significantly more affordable than the premium professional options.

Remember, it's far better to own a solid mid-range tripod than an expensive camera sitting on an unstable support.

Don’t forget to ask other club members what they have and find out what they find good and not so good about their chosen gear.

Stability Comes From Technique

Owning a good tripod is only part of the equation.

Ian also shared several practical habits that help maximise sharpness.

  • Avoid extending the centre column whenever possible.
  • Push the tripod legs firmly into sand or soft ground.
  • Check each leg is secure before opening the shutter.
  • Turn image stabilisation off when using a tripod.
  • Use a remote release or your camera's self-timer.
  • Allow any vibration to settle before taking the photograph.

One particularly clever tip was using small Velcro straps to secure the remote release cable to the tripod.

On a windy day, a cable tapping against the tripod can create enough vibration to soften a long exposure.

It's a tiny detail—but one learned through decades of experience.

Slow Down and Observe

Perhaps the greatest benefit of using a tripod isn't improved sharpness.

It's that it naturally slows you down.

Once the camera is mounted securely, you're more likely to study the edges of the frame, refine your composition and wait for the right light or the perfect wave.

Instead of taking twenty photographs in two minutes, you may make one photograph in twenty minutes.

Often, it will be the better photograph.

That deliberate approach was one of the strongest themes running through Ian's presentation.

Try It This Week

Visit a familiar location with your tripod.

Before taking your first photograph, spend five minutes doing nothing but looking.

Adjust the tripod height.
Move it a metre to the left.
Then a metre to the right.
Lower it.
Raise it.

Only when you're completely happy with the composition should you press the shutter.

Ask yourself afterwards:

Did the tripod simply hold the camera, or did it help me become a more thoughtful photographer?

You may find that slowing down is the biggest improvement of all.

💻 Editing Corner

Improve Colour & Contrast in Five Minutes (using Lightroom)

Most image editing software includes tools for adjusting exposure, contrast and colour. While this month's cheat sheet demonstrates the process using Adobe Lightroom, the same editing principles apply in many other programs, including Adobe Camera Raw, Photoshop, ON1 Photo RAW, Capture One, Luminar Neo, Affinity Photo and a range of other editing applications.

The important thing isn't the software you use—it's understanding why you're making each adjustment.

Read Improve Colour & Contrast in Five Minutes

This week's cheat sheet focuses on a simple editing workflow that can improve almost any photograph:

  1. Set the overall brightness with Exposure.
  2. Recover detail using Highlights and Shadows.
  3. Define the tonal range with Whites and Blacks.
  4. Add a touch of Contrast.
  5. Finish by enhancing colour with Vibrance, using Saturation sparingly.

Small adjustments usually produce the most natural-looking results. The aim is not to create an over-processed image, but one that is clearer, more balanced and more engaging.

Attached to this week's newsletter is a two-page beginner's cheat sheet that explains each step in more detail and provides suggested starting points for each adjustment.

📸 Photographer's Tips

🌱 Beginner Tip

Keep Your Horizons Level

A slightly crooked horizon is one of the most common distractions in landscape and seascape photography.

Most modern cameras include a built-in electronic level or grid lines to help you keep the horizon straight. If you don't have these features enabled, take an extra moment to check the horizon before pressing the shutter.

While a tilted horizon can sometimes be used creatively, a level horizon will usually produce a stronger and more polished image.

📷 Intermediate Tip

Look for Natural Leading Lines

Leading lines help guide the viewer's eye through an image towards your main subject.

Roads, fences, rivers, jetties, shorelines and even shadows can all become effective leading lines. Before taking the photograph, ask yourself where those lines are directing the viewer. If they lead out of the frame rather than towards your subject, consider changing your position.

Strong leading lines create images that feel more engaging and invite the viewer to explore the scene.

⭐ Advanced Tip

Look Beyond the Subject

Advanced photographers don't just photograph the subject—they pay close attention to everything surrounding it.

Before pressing the shutter, scan the background for bright highlights, intersecting branches, distracting shapes or objects that compete for attention. A small step to one side, a lower camera angle or a wider aperture can often simplify the scene dramatically.

Great photographs are rarely about having more in the frame. They're about ensuring every element contributes to the story.
