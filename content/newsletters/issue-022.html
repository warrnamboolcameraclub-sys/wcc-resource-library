📸 Photographing a Festival: Don’t Just Photograph the Stage

When you arrive at a festival with a camera, it is very easy to spend most of your time standing in front of the stage photographing performers.

Those photographs are important, but they are only one part of the story.

A strong set of event photographs should show what the event felt like, not just who appeared on stage.

For Saturday’s Warrnambool Multicultural Festival we will be taking a more structured approach, with photographers assigned to particular areas and then rotated during the day.

📋 Photographer Briefing for Saturday

  • Please concentrate on your assigned area as much as practical. We are deliberately spreading photographers around so we don’t all come home with photographs of the same things.
  • We will rotate areas roughly every hour. This gives everyone an opportunity to photograph different parts of the Festival.
  • Areas 1, 2 and 4 will be our main rotating assignments: Cultural Performances, Market/Stalls/Festival Atmosphere and Lighthouse Theatre Workshops.
  • Stan and Chandika will alternate VIP and Special Guest coverage.
  • After 3:00 PM, Stan and Chandika will most likely cover the Fireside Chat. If anyone else would like to stay and photograph it, please let Stan know.
  • Look beyond the obvious photograph. Include audience reactions, interactions, volunteers, details and the atmosphere around you.
  • Shoot a mixture of horizontal and vertical images.
  • Watch your backgrounds.
  • Think about the complete story.

Most importantly: if you are assigned to an area, please give that area your attention until it is time to rotate.

If something exceptional happens nearby, by all means photograph it, but the roster only works if we can rely on each area being covered.

Because everyone will rotate, nobody should be stuck photographing the same thing for the entire day.

📷 The Five Coverage Areas

1. Cultural Performances | 10 AM – 3 PM

Up to three photographers at a time will cover the performances.

  • performers in action
  • expressions and gestures
  • colourful costumes
  • movement
  • interaction between performers
  • audience reactions
  • moments before or after a performance where practical

2. Market, Stalls & Festival Atmosphere | 10 AM – 3 PM

Two photographers will concentrate on the wider Festival.

  • craft and food stalls
  • community organisations
  • children’s activities
  • people sampling food
  • conversations between stallholders and visitors
  • volunteers and festival staff
  • happy audiences
  • colourful details and general atmosphere

3. VIPs & Special Guests | 10 AM – 3 PM

Stan and Chandika will alternate coverage of the VIPs and special guests throughout the day.

These photographs are particularly important to the Warrnambool Multicultural Association because they may be used for communications, development and future partnerships.

4. Lighthouse Theatre Workshops | 10 AM – 3 PM

One photographer at a time will cover the workshops in the Lighthouse Theatre.

  • artists demonstrating their work
  • participants taking part
  • hands-on activities
  • interaction between presenters and participants
  • expressions, concentration and useful details

5. Fireside Chat | 3 PM – 5 PM

After the main Festival activities finish at 3:00 PM, there will be a Fireside Chat featuring speakers and audience discussion.

At this stage, coverage will most likely be handled by Stan and Chandika. If anyone else would like to stay after 3:00 PM and photograph the Fireside Chat as well, please let Stan know.

Don’t Forget the Audience

Some of the strongest festival photographs happen away from the main subject. Look for children watching intently, people laughing, applause, families sharing the experience and people reacting to what is happening.

Look for the Small Details

Details can add colour and variety to the final collection: hands preparing food, colourful clothing, jewellery, instruments, decorations, flags, signs, food being served, craft work and interesting textures.

Photograph Interaction, Not Just People Standing There

Rather than simply photographing someone standing behind a stall, look for the moment when something happens: a visitor asks a question, food is handed to someone, two people start laughing or a child becomes involved in an activity.

Shoot Both Horizontal and Vertical

Horizontal images are excellent for groups, performances, crowds and wider scenes. Vertical photographs are particularly useful for individual performers, portraits, costumes, people working at stalls, social media and promotional material.

Think About the Whole Story

Across the day, our combined photographs should show the Festival and crowd, performers, food, stalls, workshops, VIPs, audience reactions, volunteers, small details and the Fireside Chat.

The goal is not just to show what happened.

The goal is to show what it felt like to be there.

💾 Protecting Your Photos – Part 3

The 3-2-1 Backup Rule: Three Copies, One Less Worry

In Part 2 of our series based on Cedric Robertson’s presentation at the VAPS Convention, we explored how a single point of failure could put an entire photographic collection at risk.

This week, we’re looking at one of the most widely recommended approaches to protecting your photographs: the 3-2-1 backup rule.

The good news? You don’t need to be a computer expert or spend thousands of dollars to get started.

What Does 3-2-1 Actually Mean?

  • 3 copies of your photographs: your original working files plus two backup copies.
  • 2 independent types of storage: ideally using different storage media or technologies, such as a computer or NAS plus an external drive or cloud backup.
  • 1 copy stored offsite: somewhere physically separate from your home, so a fire, theft or other disaster cannot destroy every copy.

The idea is simple. If one device fails, you have another copy. If something happens to your home, your offsite backup provides another opportunity to recover your photographs.

What Would This Look Like for a Club Photographer?

Imagine you have a collection of 30,000 photographs stored on your computer.

Copy 1 – Your Computer

Your main collection of RAW files, edited photographs and Lightroom catalogue. This is where you organise and edit your images.

Copy 2 – An External USB Hard Drive

A separate drive containing an up-to-date backup of your photographs. Ideally, backup software runs automatically so you don’t have to remember to copy files manually.

Copy 3 – An Offsite Backup

This might be another external drive kept at a family member’s home, or an online backup service that securely stores your files away from your property.

Now, if your computer fails, you have the external drive. If a fire destroys both your computer and external drive, you still have the offsite copy.

What If You Use a NAS?

Some members use a NAS, or Network Attached Storage, with multiple hard drives configured in RAID.

While RAID can keep your storage operating after certain drive failures, RAID is not a backup.

If you accidentally delete a folder, suffer ransomware damage or lose the entire NAS, RAID alone may not save your photographs.

Your NAS can certainly be one of your three copies, but you still need independent backups.

We’ll explore RAID in more detail in Part 4.

Don’t Forget About Automatic Backups

One of the biggest problems with manual backups is remembering to do them.

Where possible, use software that automatically backs up new and changed files.

Also consider using a backup system that retains previous versions. This can help you recover a photograph that was accidentally deleted or overwritten weeks earlier.

Remember that synchronisation services aren’t necessarily backups. If a deletion or unwanted change is synchronised across every device, it may affect all your copies unless previous versions are retained.

This Week’s Practical Tip: Count Your Copies

  1. Do I have three copies of my important photographs?
  2. Are those copies stored across independent storage systems?
  3. Is at least one copy stored somewhere outside my home?

If you can’t answer yes to all three, you’ve identified an opportunity to improve your backup protection.

You don’t have to build the perfect system overnight. Start by creating another independent copy of your photographs, then work towards keeping one safely offsite.

And remember, a backup is only useful if you can restore your files from it. Periodically test your backups by recovering a few photographs and opening them.

📸 Photographer’s Tips – Set 18

🌱 Beginner Tip – B018

Don’t Miss the Moment: Use Burst Mode

Sometimes the difference between an ordinary photograph and a great one happens in a fraction of a second.

Your camera’s Continuous Shooting or Burst Mode allows you to capture several photographs in quick succession while holding down the shutter button.

This is particularly useful when photographing birds in flight, sporting events, children playing or any subject where the action changes rapidly.

Rather than relying on a single photograph, a short burst gives you several opportunities to capture the ideal expression, wing position or moment of action.

Remember that burst mode doesn’t guarantee sharp images. You’ll still need an appropriate shutter speed and autofocus settings.

Try taking a short burst of a moving subject and compare the images to see how much difference a fraction of a second can make.

📷 Intermediate Tip – I018

Use Atmospheric Perspective to Create Depth

Have you noticed how distant mountains often appear lighter, less colourful and less detailed than objects in the foreground?

This is called atmospheric perspective, and it can be a powerful compositional tool.

As light travels through the atmosphere, particles and moisture scatter it, often reducing contrast and colour saturation in distant objects.

Rather than trying to remove all that haze during editing, consider using it to your advantage.

Look for landscapes where foreground objects appear dark and detailed while distant hills gradually fade into softer tones.

This natural progression can create a wonderful sense of distance and scale.

Next time you’re photographing a landscape, look for layers of hills, trees or buildings that gradually become lighter with distance.

⭐ Advanced Tip – A018

Sharpen Selectively, Not Everything

Sharpening can improve the apparent detail in a photograph, but applying it equally across the entire image isn’t always the best approach.

Different areas of an image often benefit from different levels of sharpening.

For example, in a wildlife photograph, you may want crisp detail around the subject’s eyes and feathers while keeping the background smooth and unobtrusive.

In Lightroom or similar editing software, use masking or selective adjustments to concentrate sharpening where it contributes most to the photograph.

Be particularly careful with smooth skies, out-of-focus backgrounds and shadow areas, where excessive sharpening can emphasise noise or create unwanted texture.

The goal isn’t to make every pixel look sharp. It’s to make the important details stand out without drawing attention to the editing.
