📷 Convention Corner

This week, Ian van der Wolde's long-exposure workflow reaches the point where seconds become minutes: Bulb mode, timers and working out the correct exposure after fitting a strong ND filter.

Mastering Long Exposure Photography – Part 11: Bulb Mode, Timers and Calculating the Exposure

You've found your composition, focused carefully, checked the histogram and decided exactly how you want movement to appear in the photograph.

There's just one problem.

Your exposure needs to be two minutes.

Or perhaps five.

Maybe even sixteen.

How do you tell the camera to keep the shutter open that long, and how do you work out the correct exposure once you've put a strong ND filter over the lens?

During his VAPS Convention presentation, Ian van der Wolde explained the straightforward system he used for his traditional long exposures: Bulb mode, a locking cable release, his smartphone as a timer and an ND exposure calculator.

Once you understand the process, exposures lasting several minutes become surprisingly easy to manage.

What is Bulb Mode?

Most cameras provide a range of predetermined shutter speeds.

Depending on the camera, the longest selectable shutter speed might traditionally have been around 30 seconds, although some modern cameras allow considerably longer timed exposures.

But what happens when you need longer than your camera's normal timed shutter-speed range?

That's where Bulb mode, usually shown as B, comes in.

In Bulb mode, you control how long the shutter remains open.

With a traditional cable release, the process is simple:

Open the shutter → lock the release → wait → unlock the release → shutter closes.

If the exposure needs to last four minutes, you leave it open for four minutes.

If it needs sixteen minutes, you leave it open for sixteen minutes.

Fortunately, you don't have to stand there holding the shutter button with your finger!

Ian's Simple System

Ian described the system he used with his DSLR equipment.

He would set the camera to Bulb, attach a locking cable release and use the timer on his smartphone.

When everything was ready:

  1. Start the exposure with the cable release.
  2. Lock the release so the shutter stays open.
  3. Start the timer on the phone.
  4. Wait.
  5. When the timer sounds, unlock the cable release.
  6. Allow the camera to complete its Long Exposure Noise Reduction if enabled.

It's simple technology, but it works.

And importantly, Ian wasn't touching the camera during the exposure.

But How Do We Know How Long to Expose?

This is where ND filters initially seem complicated.

Suppose you've composed your photograph without a filter and your camera tells you the correct exposure is:

ISO 100 • f/11 • 1/125 second

Then you add a 10-stop ND filter.

The filter has dramatically reduced the amount of light reaching the sensor, so the shutter needs to remain open much longer to compensate.

For every stop of light removed, the required exposure time doubles.

Starting from 1/125 second, a 10-stop reduction takes us approximately through:

1/125 → 1/60 → 1/30 → 1/15 → 1/8 → 1/4 → 1/2 → 1 sec → 2 sec → 4 sec → 8 sec

So our new exposure is approximately:

8 seconds.

That's manageable.

But start with a slower base exposure and the numbers quickly become much larger.

When Seconds Become Minutes

Suppose your unfiltered exposure is:

1/4 second

Now add a 10-stop ND filter.

The exposure progresses approximately:

¼ → ½ → 1 → 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → 32 → 64 → 128 → 256 seconds

That's approximately:

4 minutes 16 seconds.

You could calculate that manually.

But standing beside the ocean in the dark probably isn't the best place to practise mental arithmetic.

Ian had a much easier solution.

Use an ND Calculator

During his presentation, Ian mentioned the NiSi ND Calculator as a useful smartphone tool.

The principle of these apps is straightforward.

You enter:

  • your normal exposure time
  • the strength of your ND filter

The calculator tells you the new shutter time.

If the resulting exposure is several minutes, many apps also provide a countdown timer.

That removes most of the mathematics from the process.

You can concentrate on making the photograph instead.

A Handy Reference

Here's what happens when a 10-stop ND filter is added to several starting exposures:

Normal Exposure With 10-Stop ND
1/125 sec ~8 sec
1/60 sec ~17 sec
1/30 sec ~34 sec
1/15 sec ~1 min 8 sec
1/8 sec ~2 min 8 sec
1/4 sec ~4 min 16 sec
1/2 sec ~8 min 32 sec
1 sec ~17 min 4 sec

This illustrates just how quickly a strong ND filter can move us from ordinary photography into genuinely long exposures.

Establish the Correct Exposure First

There's an important sequence to Ian's method.

Don't fit the ND filter first and then try to work everything out through a nearly black viewfinder or screen.

Start without it.

Compose your photograph.

Focus.

Set your base ISO and chosen aperture.

Establish a correct normal exposure.

Check the histogram.

Once you're happy with that photograph, you have your base exposure.

Now add the ND filter and calculate the new shutter speed.

This is one of the most useful habits we've discussed throughout this series:

Get the photograph technically right first. Then make it long.

Be Careful When Fitting the Filter

Remember Part 6?

Once you've established focus, you don't want to accidentally change it.

Strong ND filters can make autofocus difficult or impossible, particularly in low light.

So after focusing:

Switch to manual focus and leave it alone.

Then carefully fit the ND filter without touching the focusing ring or moving the camera.

Once the filter is fitted, check that the holder is properly sealed against light leaks, something we covered in Part 9.

Now you're ready.

What if Something Interrupts the Exposure?

Ian told a terrific story during his presentation about photographing a scene where boats kept passing through his composition.

Their lights would have created bright streaks across his long exposure.

His solution was wonderfully practical.

When a boat entered the scene, he covered the front of the lens.

He counted the seconds while the boat passed.

Then he uncovered the lens and continued the exposure, adding the covered time to the end.

For example, if his calculated exposure was four minutes and the lens was covered for seven seconds, he simply extended the total time by approximately seven seconds.

It demonstrates one of the interesting characteristics of very long exposures.

When you're working over several minutes, you actually have time to react to what's happening around you.

Exposure Doesn't Always Stay Constant

There's another consideration when exposures become very long around sunrise or sunset.

The light itself may be changing while the photograph is being made.

Imagine starting a 10-minute exposure shortly before sunset.

By the time the shutter closes, the sun may have disappeared and the ambient light may have fallen considerably.

Likewise, an exposure beginning before sunrise may experience increasing light during the exposure.

An ND calculator can correctly calculate the mathematical equivalent of your starting exposure.

It cannot predict exactly how natural light will change during the next ten minutes.

That's where experience, observation and occasionally a little trial and error enter the process.

Ian acknowledged that uncertainty as part of what he enjoys about long exposure photography.

You don't always know precisely what you're going to get.

Remember the Second Wait

There's one more thing to include in your planning.

As we discussed in Part 10, if Long Exposure Noise Reduction is enabled, the camera may perform a dark-frame exposure after the photograph.

So your four-minute photograph could potentially mean:

4 minutes exposing + approximately 4 minutes noise reduction = around 8 minutes

A 16-minute exposure could occupy the camera for roughly 32 minutes.

That's worth remembering when the light is changing quickly.

Sometimes you may only get one opportunity.

Modern Cameras May Make This Easier

Not every photographer will need a traditional cable release and smartphone timer.

Depending on your camera, you may have:

  • extended timed shutter speeds
  • built-in interval timers
  • dedicated long exposure modes
  • Bulb timers
  • smartphone control
  • wireless remotes

Some cameras can simply be programmed for the required exposure time.

The exact method varies enormously between manufacturers and models, so this is another occasion when spending a few minutes with your camera manual is worthwhile.

The principle, however, remains exactly the same.

Determine the exposure you need and keep the shutter open for that amount of time without disturbing the camera.

📷 Ian's Long Exposure Workflow

By this point in our series, we can bring almost everything together:

1. Set up the tripod

2. Compose without the ND filter

3. Focus carefully

4. Select base ISO

5. Choose your aperture

6. Make a normal test exposure

7. Check the histogram and highlights

8. Switch to manual focus

9. Carefully fit the ND filter

10. Check for light leaks

11. Calculate the new exposure time

12. Set Bulb mode if required

13. Secure the remote-release cable

14. Start the exposure and timer

15. Don't touch the camera

16. Close the shutter when the timer finishes

17. Allow Long Exposure Noise Reduction to finish if you're using it

It sounds like a lot when written down.

After you've done it a few times, it becomes routine.

And that's precisely why Ian stressed the importance of becoming familiar with your equipment.

📷 Try It This Week

You don't need to make a 16-minute exposure.

Start with something manageable.

Find a scene containing moving water or clouds and establish a normal exposure.

Then use an ND filter and an exposure calculator to aim for approximately:

30 seconds

Take the photograph.

Now, if conditions allow, adjust your filter strength or starting exposure and try:

2 minutes

Compare the results.

Did the additional exposure time improve the photograph?

What happened to the water?

What happened to the clouds?

Did distracting movement disappear?

Most importantly:

Which photograph do you prefer?

Remember, the objective isn't to achieve the longest exposure.

It's to choose the exposure time that creates the image you want.

💡 Quick Tip

Don't try to calculate a 10-stop ND exposure in your head while standing on a beach in the dark.

Establish your correct exposure without the filter, then use an ND exposure calculator to determine the filtered exposure.

Ian specifically mentioned the NiSi ND Calculator during his presentation.

And remember:

30 seconds isn't automatically better than 5 seconds, and 5 minutes isn't automatically better than 30 seconds.

The shutter speed is a creative decision.

We've spent eleven parts learning how to make long exposures.

Perhaps the most important lesson is knowing when not to make one.

💻 Editing Corner

Sometimes a photograph is almost right except for one small thing. This week we look at using Lightroom Classic's removal tools without cleaning away the story of the photograph.

Lightroom Basics – Part 10: Removing Distractions

Sometimes a photograph is almost right, except for one small thing.

It might be:

  • A rubbish bin at the edge of the frame
  • A person in the background
  • A bright sign
  • A pole or wire
  • A spot on the sensor
  • A small object that keeps pulling your eye away from the subject

Lightroom Classic's Remove tools are designed for exactly this sort of cleanup. Current versions include Remove, Heal, Clone, and AI-powered Generative Remove options.

The important principle is:

Remove distractions that weaken the photograph, not things simply because Lightroom can remove them.

1. Start by Looking at the Edges

Before opening any tool, look around all four edges of the photograph.

Ask yourself:

  • Is anything half-cut-off?
  • Is there a bright object competing with the subject?
  • Is something entering the frame unnecessarily?
  • Could a simple crop remove the problem instead?

Sometimes Part 2: Cropping for Better Composition is still the easiest answer.

There is no point spending several minutes removing an object if a small crop solves the problem cleanly.

2. Open the Remove Tool

In Lightroom Classic's Develop module, select the Remove tool.

The traditional shortcut for Lightroom's removal tools is:

Q

Lightroom offers several approaches depending on the job.

Remove

Designed to remove an unwanted area and replace it with surrounding image content.

Heal

Uses information from another part of the photograph while blending texture, lighting and shading into the surrounding area.

Clone

Copies pixels directly from another part of the photograph.

Adobe describes Heal as blending the sampled area into its new surroundings, while Clone directly duplicates the source pixels.

3. Start Small

Small distractions are usually the easiest place to begin.

Examples include:

  • Dust spots
  • Small rubbish
  • A twig crossing an edge
  • A distant sign
  • Tiny bright objects

Make the brush only slightly larger than the object you want to remove.

Paint over the distraction and then inspect the result.

Beginner rule

The smaller and simpler the removal, the easier it is for Lightroom to produce a natural result.

4. Try Generative Remove for Larger Objects

For more complicated distractions, Lightroom Classic now includes Generative Remove, powered by Adobe Firefly.

Adobe says it can remove objects such as vehicles, signs and poles and generate replacement content that blends with the rest of the photograph.

To use it:

  1. Open Remove
  2. Select Use Generative AI
  3. Brush over the unwanted object
  4. Select Remove
  5. Review the generated variations

Lightroom normally generates several alternatives so you can choose the most convincing result.

5. Let Lightroom Detect the Object

With Detect Objects, you do not necessarily need to paint perfectly around every edge.

You can loosely paint or circle the object and Lightroom attempts to identify the object itself, including associated shadows or reflections where appropriate.

This can be particularly useful for:

  • Signs
  • People
  • Cars
  • Poles
  • Bags
  • Other clearly defined objects

If Lightroom misses part of the object, refine the selection using Add or Subtract.

6. Removing Background People

Recent versions of Lightroom Classic also include a dedicated People option within Distraction Removal.

Lightroom analyses the photograph, identifies background people and places individual selection pins over them. You can exclude anyone you want to keep before Lightroom generates the removal.

This can be useful for:

  • Travel photographs
  • Street scenes
  • Events
  • Landscapes with accidental passers-by

But think before you use it.

If the people are an important part of the story of the photograph, removing them may actually make the image weaker.

7. Check the Result at 100%

Removal tools can look convincing when viewing the full photograph, but problems may become obvious when you zoom in.

Check for:

  • Repeated patterns
  • Blurred patches
  • Strange textures
  • Bent lines
  • Duplicated objects
  • Unnatural edges

This is especially important around:

  • Brickwork
  • Fences
  • Water
  • Architecture
  • Repeating patterns
  • Fine foliage

If the result looks suspicious, try another generated variation or redo the selection.

Heal or Clone?

There are still times when the traditional tools are the better choice.

Use Heal when:

  • Removing sensor spots
  • Fixing blemishes
  • Cleaning small imperfections
  • You want Lightroom to blend the replacement naturally

Use Clone when:

  • You need an exact copy of another area
  • The surrounding texture must remain consistent
  • Heal is producing an unwanted blend

Adobe notes that Clone duplicates the sampled pixels while Heal blends texture, lighting and shading with the target area.

Don't Remove Too Much

This is where editing judgement becomes important.

Once you discover how effective removal tools can be, it is tempting to clean everything.

But some elements provide:

  • Context
  • Scale
  • Atmosphere
  • Location
  • Story

A person walking along a beach may be distracting in one photograph but essential in another.

A road sign may be ugly, but it might also explain where the photograph was taken.

The objective is not to create an empty, perfect world.

It is to strengthen the photograph.

A Simple Beginner Workflow

When something is distracting:

  1. Ask whether a small crop would solve it
  2. Open Remove
  3. Start with the smallest distraction
  4. Try Remove/Heal for simple areas
  5. Use Generative Remove for more complex objects
  6. Use Detect Objects where appropriate
  7. Check the result at 100%
  8. Try another variation if the fill looks unnatural
  9. Compare with the original
  10. Stop when the distraction is no longer drawing attention
Final Check

Ask yourself:

  • Was the object genuinely distracting?
  • Does the repaired area look believable?
  • Are there repeated textures or obvious patches?
  • Have I removed anything important to the story?
  • Would a crop have been simpler?
  • Does the photograph still look natural?

If you find yourself noticing the repaired area, it probably still needs work.

Remember

The best removal is one nobody notices.

Removing distractions should simplify the photograph and strengthen the subject, not make the viewer wonder what was edited.

📸 Photographer's Tips

🌱 Beginner Tip – B013

Don't Always Trust Auto White Balance

Auto White Balance does a good job most of the time, but it doesn't always produce the look you want.

Your camera may try to neutralise warm light at sunrise or sunset, reducing some of the colour that made you want to take the photograph in the first place.

Try changing from Auto to settings such as Daylight, Cloudy or Shade and watch how the colours change.

If you shoot RAW, you can adjust white balance later with much more flexibility, but learning to recognise different types of light while you're taking the photograph is still a valuable skill.

📷 Intermediate Tip – I013

Try Panning With a Moving Subject

Not every action photograph needs to freeze movement.

Panning involves following a moving subject with your camera while using a slower shutter speed. If you match the movement successfully, your subject can remain relatively sharp while the background becomes streaked with motion.

Start with something predictable, such as a cyclist or car travelling across your position. Follow the subject smoothly, press the shutter while continuing to move the camera, and keep following through after the exposure.

Experiment with different shutter speeds. There is no single correct setting because the speed and distance of your subject will affect the result.

⭐ Advanced Tip – A013

Think in High Key and Low Key

Exposure doesn't always have to produce a photograph with an even spread of tones.

A high-key image is dominated by lighter tones, while a low-key image is dominated by darker tones. Both can be used deliberately to create mood and simplify a composition.

The important distinction is intent. A high-key photograph isn't simply overexposed, and a low-key photograph isn't simply underexposed. Important highlight or shadow detail should be controlled according to what you want the image to communicate.

Next time you encounter a very bright or very dark scene, consider working with its tonal character rather than trying to make everything look average.

📷 This Week's Camera Challenge

Think High Key

This week's challenge ties directly into Thursday night's High Key Photography Workshop.

Your aim is to create a photograph dominated by light tones, with minimal dark areas and a bright, clean overall feel.

Good subjects might include a person against a bright background, a flower on white, a simple object photographed in a light box, pale textures or fabrics, a bright interior, or a subject lit strongly enough that the background becomes very light.

The important thing is not simply to overexpose the photograph. A successful high-key image should still have shape, detail and control in the areas that matter.

Try This

  1. Choose a subject that will work against a bright or simple background.
  2. Keep the overall image predominantly light.
  3. Watch the histogram and highlights so important detail is not lost accidentally.
  4. Look carefully at any dark elements and decide whether they help or distract.
  5. Compare a normal exposure with a deliberately high-key version.

Bonus Challenge

Try making two high-key photographs: one using a person and one using a small object, flower or still-life subject.

The aim is not to make the photograph as bright as possible. The aim is to make brightness part of the design.
