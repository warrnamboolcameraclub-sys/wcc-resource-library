🏃 Our Members Out in Force at the Running Festival

Warrnambool Camera Club members were out in force across the weekend, helping record the many stories of the Warrnambool Running Festival.

The event was organised by the Warrnambool Running Club.

Six Club photographers volunteered on Saturday, followed by eleven on Sunday.

Sunday's program ran from 7:00 AM until midday, with some competitors still out on the course as they battled the warm and windy conditions.

Our photographers covered runners around the course and at the finish, capturing the effort, emotion and atmosphere of the event.

A Dramatic Marathon Finish

One of the most difficult moments came in the final 30 metres of the Marathon. The runner who had led the race physically collapsed as his legs and body could no longer carry him forward.

He managed to continue and cross the finish line unassisted, but not before the second-placed runner had passed him. The eventual winner did not initially realise he had won, believing the other runner had already finished before collapsing.

It was a heartbreaking finish for the long-time leader and an extraordinary display of determination as he made his way across the line.

📷 Convention Corner

This week, our series based on Ian van der Wolde's presentation at this year's VAPS Convention explains why a long exposure can leave your camera working for twice as long as expected.

Mastering Long Exposure Photography – Part 10: Long Exposure Noise Reduction – Why Your Camera Makes You Wait Twice

You've carefully set up your composition and started a 10-minute exposure.

Ten minutes later the shutter closes.

You look at the camera expecting to see your photograph.

Instead, the camera appears to be busy for another ten minutes.

Nothing seems to be happening.

But something very important is.

During his VAPS Convention presentation, Ian van der Wolde explained why he uses Long Exposure Noise Reduction and why, with conventional long exposures, a 16-minute photograph could effectively tie up his camera for 32 minutes.

That second 16 minutes isn't another photograph. The camera is creating what is commonly called a dark frame, and its job is to help clean up the first one.

Why Do Long Exposures Create Problems?

Digital sensors generate electronic noise, and during a very long exposure the sensor and camera electronics can warm up.

As the exposure becomes longer, unwanted artefacts can become more noticeable, particularly in darker areas of the image. These may include:

  • hot or bright pixels
  • fixed-pattern noise
  • coloured specks
  • other sensor-related artefacts

This is very different from simply using a high ISO.

Remember from Part 8 that Ian generally starts his long exposure photography around his camera's base ISO. But even at base ISO, keeping the sensor active for many minutes can introduce long-exposure artefacts.

That's where Long Exposure Noise Reduction comes in.

What Does the Camera Actually Do?

Suppose you make a five-minute exposure.

The camera records your photograph normally. Then, with Long Exposure Noise Reduction enabled, it makes another exposure of approximately the same duration, but without recording the scene.

The shutter remains closed, so this second exposure should essentially be black. However, the sensor may still record hot pixels and other electronic artefacts.

The camera can then compare this dark frame with the original photograph and use it to identify and suppress those unwanted artefacts. This process is generally known as dark-frame subtraction.

5-minute photograph + approximately 5-minute dark frame = around 10 minutes before you're ready to shoot again.

Ian described the same thing with some of his extremely long exposures:

16-minute photograph + 16-minute dark frame = approximately 32 minutes.

Suddenly, long exposure photography really does become a lesson in patience.

Why Can't the Camera Just Fix It Later?

Some long-exposure artefacts are related to the sensor's behaviour at the time the photograph was taken.

The dark frame gives the camera a reference made under closely comparable conditions.

That's why Ian made another particularly important point during his presentation:

Don't dramatically change the camera's environment while it's making the dark frame.

Don't Put the Camera Straight Into a Warm Car

Imagine you've just finished a 16-minute exposure on a freezing winter evening.

The photograph finishes and Long Exposure Noise Reduction begins. You're cold. The car is nearby.

It might be tempting to pick up the camera, put it into the warm car and drive home while it finishes processing.

Ian specifically warned against doing this because it changes the camera's thermal environment. The photograph was made with the camera sitting outside in the cold. Moving it immediately into a heated car changes the conditions under which the dark frame is being recorded.

For dark-frame subtraction to be most representative, you want that second exposure made under conditions reasonably similar to those of the original photograph.

Ian's approach was simple:

Leave the camera in the shooting environment while the process finishes.

You can retreat to the car yourself if necessary. Just don't warm the camera up halfway through its work.

This Explains Ian's Patience

Earlier in this series we discussed something that initially sounds almost absurd. Ian might spend half an hour making one photograph.

Now we can see why.

14-minute exposure + 14-minute dark frame = 28 minutes.

If you decide you'd like another photograph, you start again.

Photography at this pace is completely different from walking around taking dozens of images. Ian spoke about how much he enjoys precisely that aspect of long exposure photography.

It forces him to slow down. While the camera is exposing, he has time to look around, think about the composition and consider what he might photograph next.

In fact, he explained that some of his later photographs were discovered while he was simply waiting for another long exposure to finish.

Should Everyone Turn Long Exposure Noise Reduction On?

This is where it's worth understanding your own camera.

Ian's advice during the presentation was clear: he used Long Exposure Noise Reduction with his conventional long exposures.

However, camera technology and photographers' workflows vary.

Some photographers prefer to make their own dark frames and process them later. Modern RAW-processing software can also deal effectively with many types of noise and hot pixels, and some newer cameras may handle long exposures differently.

Check your camera manual for:

  • when the function activates
  • whether the dark frame matches the original exposure time
  • whether you can take another photograph while processing occurs
  • whether the feature operates with every shutter mode
  • how your particular camera handles very long exposures

For someone beginning with long exposure photography, Ian's approach provides a very straightforward starting point:

Enable Long Exposure Noise Reduction and allow the camera to complete the process.

Don't Confuse Two Types of Noise Reduction

Your camera may have settings for both:

High ISO Noise Reduction

and

Long Exposure Noise Reduction.

They're not the same thing.

High ISO Noise Reduction is primarily concerned with noise associated with images made at higher ISO settings, particularly JPEG processing.

Long Exposure Noise Reduction addresses artefacts associated with long exposure times, commonly through dark-frame subtraction.

For this series, it's the second one we're interested in.

Look through your camera's menu and see whether you can find Long Exposure NR or Long Exposure Noise Reduction.

There's a Trade-Off

Long Exposure Noise Reduction may improve the file, but you pay for it with something valuable:

time.

Imagine you're photographing an extraordinary sunset. You make a four-minute exposure. Then the camera needs approximately another four minutes for its dark frame.

During those eight minutes, the light could change dramatically.

That's one reason it's important to think about the photograph before committing to an extremely long exposure.

Ian also recommended making a normal exposure first. Check:

Composition → Focus → Exposure → Histogram

Get those things right before committing several minutes to the final photograph. You don't want to wait 20 minutes only to discover that your horizon wasn't level.

What About Ian's Phase One?

Ian also explained why his current Phase One system has changed the way he works.

His camera offers a feature called Frame Averaging.

Rather than making one conventional exposure through a very strong ND filter, the camera can capture a rapid sequence of shorter exposures and computationally combine them into a single RAW file.

Ian explained that this allows him to create the visual effect of a very long exposure without using the traditional ND-filter workflow he previously relied upon.

It also gives him an interesting advantage. If conditions suddenly change, he can stop the process and retain the exposure accumulated to that point rather than necessarily losing the entire photograph.

It's impressive technology, although, as Ian joked during his presentation, you might need to mortgage the family to buy one!

Fortunately, none of this specialist equipment is necessary to enjoy long exposure photography. A camera, tripod, ND filter and a little patience can take you a very long way.

📷 Try It This Week

Before heading out, find Long Exposure Noise Reduction in your camera's menu. If you're not sure what it does on your particular model, check the manual.

Then make a controlled experiment. Set the camera securely on a tripod and make a long exposure with Long Exposure Noise Reduction enabled.

Watch what happens when the photograph finishes:

  • Does the camera display a processing message?
  • How long does it take?
  • Can you use the camera while it's processing?

Then inspect the resulting RAW file at 100% magnification, particularly in the darker areas.

💻 Editing Corner

A crooked horizon is one of the easiest problems to fix in Lightroom, and one of the quickest ways to make a photograph look more polished.

Lightroom Basics – Part 9: Straightening Horizons

A crooked horizon is especially noticeable in:

  • seascapes
  • landscapes
  • lakes and rivers
  • architecture
  • photographs with obvious horizontal lines

Even a small tilt can make the entire photograph feel slightly wrong.

The good news is that Lightroom Classic gives you several simple ways to correct it.

1. Open the Crop Tool

In Lightroom Classic, open the photograph in the Develop module.

Then select the Crop tool.

You can also press:

R

to open Crop directly. Adobe's current Lightroom Classic shortcut reference confirms that R selects the Crop tool.

2. Try Auto First

For a photograph with an obvious horizon, Lightroom may be able to straighten it automatically.

Select Auto.

Lightroom analyses the photograph and rotates it to correct the tilt.

This is a useful first step, particularly for simple landscapes and seascapes.

But always check the result yourself.

Automatic does not necessarily mean correct.

3. Use the Angle Tool

For more control, use the Angle tool in Crop & Straighten.

Select the Angle tool, then draw a line directly along something in the photograph that you know should be horizontal.

For example:

  • the sea horizon
  • the edge of a lake
  • a building roofline
  • a fence line that you know is level

When you release the mouse, Lightroom rotates the photograph so that the line you drew becomes level.

Beginner rule: Tell Lightroom what should be level rather than guessing by eye.

4. Use the Angle Slider

You can also straighten manually with the Angle slider.

Move the slider gradually until the photograph looks level.

This can be useful where there is no single obvious horizon line.

5. Use the Grid

When you straighten a photograph, Lightroom displays a grid over the image.

Use it to compare major lines against the horizontal and vertical guides.

The grid is particularly useful for:

  • buildings
  • doorways
  • windows
  • poles
  • architecture
  • interiors

You can also manually rotate the photograph by dragging outside the crop boundary while using the grid as a guide.

Be Careful With Natural Horizons

Not every line in a landscape is genuinely horizontal.

A shoreline can curve. A hill can slope. A road can rise. A fence can run downhill.

If you straighten the photograph against the wrong reference line, you may actually make it look worse.

For seascapes, the distant sea horizon is normally the most reliable reference because the apparent horizon should be level unless perspective or lens distortion is affecting the image.

Straightening Will Crop the Photograph

When Lightroom rotates an image to straighten it, the corners of the photograph move outside the rectangular frame.

As a result, Lightroom must usually crop away a small amount of the image.

This is another reason to avoid excessive correction. A small camera tilt generally causes only a small crop. A large correction can remove a significant part of the photograph.

Watch the Edges After Straightening

Once the horizon is level, look around the edges again. Straightening may have changed the composition.

Ask:

  • Has something important been cropped?
  • Is the subject now too close to an edge?
  • Has the balance of the composition changed?
  • Do I need to adjust the crop slightly?

This links directly back to Part 2: Cropping for Better Composition.

Straightening and cropping often work together.

What About Buildings?

Buildings introduce another issue: perspective.

If you point the camera upward at a tall building, the vertical lines may appear to lean inward.

Simply rotating the photograph will not necessarily fix this.

Lightroom Classic's Transform panel provides Upright options for correcting horizontal and vertical perspective, including Auto, Level, Vertical, Full and Guided corrections. Adobe explains these options in its Upright perspective-correction guide.

For this Lightroom Basics exercise, however, concentrate first on getting the photograph level. Perspective correction is a slightly different job.

A Simple Beginner Workflow

When you notice a crooked photograph:

  1. Press R to open Crop.
  2. Try Auto.
  3. If needed, select the Angle tool.
  4. Draw along a line that should be level.
  5. Use the grid to check the result.
  6. Fine-tune with the Angle slider.
  7. Check all four edges.
  8. Adjust the crop if necessary.
  9. Compare before and after.
Don't Obsess Over Tiny Differences

It is possible to spend far too long moving the Angle slider by fractions of a degree.

The objective is not to achieve a mathematically perfect number. It is to make the photograph look level.

For a strong sea horizon or architectural line, accuracy matters.

For a natural landscape with no obvious horizontal reference, visual balance is often more important than chasing a particular angle.

Final Check

  • Does the horizon look level?
  • Are important vertical lines believable?
  • Did I use a genuine reference line?
  • Has straightening damaged the composition?
  • Did Lightroom crop anything important?
  • Does the photograph now feel more balanced?

If the tilt is no longer noticeable, stop.

A straight horizon should disappear into the photograph. A crooked one attracts attention immediately.

📸 Photographer's Tips

🌱 Beginner – B012

Check Your Background

It is easy to become so focused on your subject that you don't notice what is happening behind it.

Before pressing the shutter, quickly check the background for bright objects, people, signs, poles or branches that might distract from your subject.

Often you don't need to change lenses or camera settings. Moving a step to the left or right, getting a little lower or changing your angle can give you a much cleaner background.

The background is part of your photograph too.

📷 Intermediate – I012

Change Your Height, Change the Photograph

Most photographs are taken from standing eye level simply because that's where the camera happens to be.

Try getting lower, shooting from ground level or finding a higher viewpoint. Changing your height alters the relationship between your subject, foreground and background and can completely transform an otherwise ordinary scene.

This is particularly effective with children, animals, landscapes and street photography.

Before leaving a scene, ask yourself: What would this look like from somewhere other than here?

⭐ Advanced – A012

Control Contrast, Don't Just Accept It

High contrast scenes can force the viewer's attention towards areas you never intended.

Before taking the photograph, identify the brightest highlights and deepest shadows and consider how they contribute to the image. Sometimes changing your position, waiting for softer light or exposing differently will create a better balance.

At other times, strong contrast is exactly what the photograph needs.

The important thing is that contrast should be a deliberate part of the image, rather than something you discover later when you start editing.

📷 This Week's Camera Challenge

Keep It Level

This week's challenge connects directly with our Lightroom Basics article.

Find a scene containing a strong horizontal or vertical reference. It could be a sea horizon, lake edge, roofline, doorway or another line that should clearly appear level.

  1. Take time to level the camera before making the photograph.
  2. Review the image carefully rather than relying only on how it looked through the viewfinder.
  3. If necessary, use Lightroom's Angle tool to straighten it.
  4. Check what was lost around the edges when the image was rotated and cropped.

The aim is simple: make the photograph feel naturally balanced, without the viewer noticing the correction.
