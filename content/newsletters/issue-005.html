Judge's Takeaway

One of the recurring themes throughout Lin's judging was the importance of strong technical execution. Sharpness, thoughtful composition and careful cropping featured regularly in his comments.

Every judge brings a different perspective to a competition. Some place greater emphasis on technical excellence, while others are drawn more strongly to concept, storytelling or emotional impact. Listening carefully to those recurring themes and applying them to our own photography is one of the best ways to continue improving.

🚶 Photography Walk for Wellness Update

Around 16–17 photographers joined us for this month's Photography Walk for Wellness.

With this month's destination centred on the Warrnambool Library, our walk ran from 10:00 am, giving everyone the opportunity to explore both inside and around the library precinct.

Our optional theme for the morning was Letters, encouraging members to see familiar surroundings from a different perspective.

Wally set himself the ambitious challenge of photographing every letter of the alphabet using naturally occurring shapes and objects, while I spent the morning searching for enough letterforms to spell WARRNAMBOOL.

Others simply photographed whatever inspired them, which is exactly what these walks are all about. The monthly theme is there purely as a creative prompt for anyone looking for a different challenge.

Afterwards, everyone gathered at Fish Tales Café for coffee, cake and plenty of conversation.

For me, that's always one of the highlights of the morning. Photography brings us together, but it's the friendships, conversations and shared enthusiasm over coffee that keep people coming back.

✂️ New Initiative – Print Mat Cutting Sessions

For some time we've talked about running regular mat cutting sessions to help members prepare prints for competition.

I'm pleased to say we're now actively moving forward with the idea.

Preparing prints for competition can seem daunting if you don't have access to a quality mat cutter or haven't mounted a print before. We know this can be one of the barriers preventing members from entering our Print competitions.

These sessions will provide members with the opportunity to measure borders, cut mat board to size and use a professional mat cutter to complete the final window, with experienced members on hand to help throughout the process.

The current plan is to run small group sessions around a large work table, giving everyone plenty of room to prepare and trim their mat boards before using the cutter. We think this will provide a relaxed environment where members can learn from one another while gaining confidence in preparing prints for competition.

More details will be announced soon, but we hope this initiative encourages even more members to give the Print sections a try.

📷 Convention Corner

Ethical Bird Photography

The Four F's: Spectacular Images at What Cost?

During her VAPS Convention presentation, Kim Wormald introduced a simple framework that quickly resonated with the audience: The Four F's.

These are the types of bird photographs that naturally attract attention, perform well in competitions and often receive thousands of likes on social media:

  • Feeding
  • Fighting
  • Flying
  • F... (breeding behaviour)

There is nothing inherently wrong with photographing any of these behaviours. In fact, when captured naturally they can produce some of the most spectacular wildlife images a photographer will ever make.

The problem begins when photographers try to manufacture the moment rather than patiently waiting for it to occur.

Kim shared examples where photographers deliberately influence bird behaviour to create dramatic images. Birds may be flushed from their perch to capture a flight shot. Territorial calls may be played through speakers to provoke an aggressive response. Birds may be lured with food, while predators are sometimes baited to produce hunting photographs.

Other practices include removing branches around nests for a cleaner composition, repeatedly using flash on nocturnal species, or publicising the location of rare birds, leading to increased pressure from photographers on already vulnerable wildlife.

Many of these practices are well known within the bird photography community. Others occur simply because photographers are unaware of the consequences. As Kim reminded us, not every unethical photograph is taken with bad intentions. Education is often the most effective solution.

One of the strongest messages from the presentation was simple:

The welfare of the bird must always come before the photograph.

A spectacular image may be admired for a few seconds on social media, but the consequences for the bird can last much longer. A disturbed nest may be abandoned. A breeding bird may expend valuable energy defending what it believes is a real intruder. A bird repeatedly flushed into flight loses energy it needs simply to survive.

As photographers, we all enjoy capturing those once-in-a-lifetime moments. The challenge is ensuring they remain genuine moments rather than scenes we have created ourselves. Look out for the next article in this series as it explores an alternative approach to the four F's.

📸 This Week's Camera Challenge

The next time you're photographing birds or wildlife, pause for a moment before pressing the shutter.

Ask yourself:

  • Am I changing this animal's behaviour?
  • Would this moment have happened if I wasn't here?
  • Can I achieve the photograph simply by waiting a little longer?

Sometimes the best wildlife photograph is the one we choose not to take.
