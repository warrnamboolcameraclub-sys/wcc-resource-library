📷 Convention Corner

Mastering Long Exposure Photography

Part 5: Neutral Density Filters – Unlocking the Magic of Long Exposure

"An ND filter doesn't make you a better photographer. It simply gives you the creative freedom to use shutter speeds that would otherwise be impossible."

– Inspired by Ian van der Wolde's VAPS Convention presentation

By now we've explored why photographers are drawn to long exposure photography and the importance of a stable tripod. The next piece of the puzzle is understanding the tool that makes many of those beautiful daytime long exposures possible – the Neutral Density (ND) filter.

If you've ever wondered how photographers create silky waterfalls, mist-like waves or clouds stretched across the sky in the middle of the day, the answer is usually an ND filter.

Read Part 5: Neutral Density Filters

What is an ND Filter?

Think of an ND filter as a pair of sunglasses for your camera.

It reduces the amount of light entering the lens without changing the colour of the scene. With less light reaching the camera sensor, you can use a much slower shutter speed while maintaining the correct exposure.

For example, without an ND filter on a sunny day your camera might suggest ISO 100, f/11 and 1/125 second. Add a 10-stop ND filter, and that same exposure becomes approximately ISO 100, f/11 and 8 seconds.

Nothing else has changed except the shutter speed, and that changes the appearance of movement completely.

Why Use One?

The real purpose of an ND filter isn't simply to make longer exposures. It's to control how movement is recorded.

Moving water becomes smooth. Waves merge into mist. Clouds stretch across the sky. Crowds disappear from busy streets. Even rough seas can take on a calm, peaceful appearance.

Ian demonstrated this beautifully during his presentation, showing how a chaotic coastal scene filled with birds and choppy water was transformed into a quiet, minimalist landscape simply by extending the exposure.

Choosing Your First ND Filter

Filter Reduces Light By Typical Use
3 Stop (ND8) 3 stops Softening waterfalls, rivers and moving water in overcast conditions.
6 Stop (ND64) 6 stops A versatile choice for waterfalls, beaches and coastal photography.
10 Stop (ND1000) 10 stops The classic choice for dramatic long exposures during daylight.
15 Stop 15 stops Extremely long exposures, often several minutes in bright daylight.

For most photographers starting out, Ian suggested that a 10-stop ND filter provides the greatest creative flexibility.

Screw-In or Square Filters?

Screw-In Filters are compact, affordable and quick to use, though you may need step-up rings or multiple sizes.

Square Filter Systems can be used across multiple lenses and make it easier to combine filters, though they are bulkier and more expensive.

Ian mentioned that if using screw-in filters, it often makes sense to buy the filter size that fits your largest lens and then use inexpensive step-up rings for smaller lenses.

Filter Quality Matters

One of Ian's strongest pieces of advice was not to compromise on filter quality.

Inferior filters can reduce sharpness, create colour casts or introduce unwanted reflections. While quality filters cost more initially, they are a long-term investment that can last for many years.

Don't Forget About Light Leaks

Long exposures reveal problems that normal photography often hides.

Even tiny amounts of light entering around a filter holder can fog part of an image during a multi-minute exposure. Ian also reminded DSLR users to cover the rear viewfinder during long exposures, as light entering through the viewfinder can affect the final image.

Remember… Filters Don't Create Great Photographs

A long exposure should serve the image, not become the subject of the image.

Sometimes a two-second exposure is perfect. Sometimes thirty seconds works better. Occasionally no ND filter is required at all.

The photographer's job is to decide what best tells the story of the scene.

Try It This Week

If you own an ND filter, visit a local beach, river or waterfall and photograph the same composition using several different shutter speeds.

Try approximately 1 second, 5 seconds, 15 seconds and 30 seconds.

Compare the images and ask yourself which exposure best suits the scene, when the water becomes too smooth, and whether the longer exposure improves the photograph or simply makes it different.

If you don't yet own an ND filter, head out just before sunrise, after sunset or on an overcast day and experiment with naturally slower shutter speeds.

Ian's Field Kit

  • Quality ND filters
  • Step-up rings to fit multiple lenses
  • Light-tight filter holder
  • Remote release
  • Lens cloth

Practical Tip: Buy one quality filter rather than several cheap ones. A good ND filter can serve you for many years and across multiple camera systems.

💻 Editing Corner

Lightroom Basics — Part 3

Understanding the Histogram

The histogram can look complicated when you first open Lightroom, but it is actually a very useful visual guide. It shows how the tones in your photograph are distributed, from the darkest areas through to the brightest.

The important thing to remember is that there is no single “correct” histogram shape.

Read Understanding the Histogram

How to Read the Histogram

Left side = blacks and shadows
Middle = midtones
Right side = highlights and whites

The height of the graph simply tells you how many pixels fall into each tonal range.

A Simple Lightroom Process

1. Look at the photograph first
Ask whether the photograph appears too dark, too bright, or reasonably balanced. The histogram should help confirm what you are seeing rather than replace your judgement.

2. Read the histogram from left to right
If most of the graph is toward the left, the photograph contains a lot of darker tones. If most of it is toward the right, the photograph contains a lot of brighter tones.

3. Pay attention to the edges
The areas to watch most carefully are the extreme left and extreme right. Lightroom provides clipping indicators at the top corners of the histogram.

4. Turn on the clipping warning
Press J to show or hide the shadow and highlight clipping warnings. Blue indicates clipped shadows and red indicates clipped highlights.

5. Make your tonal adjustments
Use Exposure, Highlights, Shadows, Whites and Blacks while watching both the photograph and the histogram.

Don't Try to Make a Perfect Mountain

A common beginner mistake is trying to make every histogram evenly spread across the whole graph.

The histogram describes the photograph; it does not score it.

When Clipping Can Be Okay

A photograph containing the sun, a bright reflection, or a completely black background may legitimately contain some areas with no recoverable detail.

The question is whether important detail has been lost.

Final Check

  • Does the photograph look correctly exposed?
  • Can I see detail where I expect to see it?
  • Have important highlights become pure white?
  • Have important shadows become completely black?
  • Does the tonal range suit the scene?

Useful shortcut: J — Show or hide the shadow and highlight clipping warnings.

Although we're demonstrating these techniques using Adobe Lightroom, the principles apply to many other photo-editing programs.

📸 Photographer's Tips

🌱 Beginner Tip

Check Your Shutter Speed

A photograph can be perfectly focused and still look soft if your shutter speed is too slow.

As a simple exercise, photograph the same moving subject at several shutter speeds and compare the results.

📷 Intermediate Tip

Use Negative Space

Sometimes giving your subject more empty space can make it stronger.

Try placing a small subject away from the centre of the frame and allowing the empty space to become part of the composition.

⭐ Advanced Tip

Watch Where the Brightest Area Falls

The viewer's eye is naturally attracted to bright areas, particularly when they contrast strongly with their surroundings.

Controlling brightness isn't simply about exposure. It's also about controlling where the viewer looks.

📷 This Week's Camera Challenge

Find the Foreground

This week, when you're photographing a landscape, don't just concentrate on what's happening in the distance. Look at what's immediately in front of you.

Find something that can become part of the composition, such as a rock, plant, pathway, fence, puddle, piece of driftwood or interesting texture, and use it to help lead the viewer into the photograph.

Try taking two versions of the same scene:

  • One without anything significant in the foreground.
  • One where you've deliberately incorporated a foreground element.

The challenge: Create a landscape where the foreground is an important part of the composition rather than something you happened to include.
