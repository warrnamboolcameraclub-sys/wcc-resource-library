📋 You Spoke – We Listened: Our 2027 Member Survey

A big thank you to everyone who took the time to complete our 2027 Member Survey.

The response has given the committee a great deal of useful information about what members enjoy, what you would like to see more of and where we can continue to improve.

The overall feedback was very positive, but just as importantly, there were plenty of constructive ideas that we can put to practical use.

We didn't conduct the survey simply to collect numbers. Your feedback will help shape what we do during 2027.

More Opportunities to Get Out With Our Cameras

One of the clearest messages was that members enjoy the social side of photography.

When members described what they enjoy about our social activities, recurring themes included friendship, connecting with other members, visiting new places and learning new genres and techniques together.

And when we asked what you would like more of, photo walks came out on top, followed closely by guest speakers and weekend workshops.

That's a message we've heard loud and clear.

We will be looking for more opportunities during 2027 to get out with our cameras, both through organised Club activities and through more informal opportunities for members to photograph together.

Our Private Facebook Group Will Have a New Purpose

The Club already has a private Facebook group that was set up some years ago, but it has never really been used and most members would not even know it exists.

We now plan to put it to practical use.

Importantly, this will not become another place where you have to look for official Club information. The weekly newsletter will remain our primary way of keeping members informed.

Instead, think of the private Facebook group as an informal members’ clubhouse.

If you’re heading out with your camera and wouldn’t mind some company, put up a post:

“I’m heading down to Thunder Point for sunset tonight. Anyone interested in joining me?”

Perhaps you’ve heard there are whales at Logans Beach, you’re heading to Tower Hill for some wildlife photography, there’s an interesting event happening around town, or you’re simply going for a walk with your camera.

Let other members know. Someone else might be thinking exactly the same thing.

These do not have to be organised Camera Club outings. There does not need to be a committee member involved and nobody needs to prepare an itinerary.

It is simply about making it easier for members to get together and enjoy photography.

The group will be private and restricted to Club members. We’ll explain more about how it will work, how to join and how to manage notifications at this week’s Club gathering.

We’ll also be sending invitations to members where we can, but there may be a few people we miss. If you would like to join the group and haven’t received an invitation, please let us know so we can add you.

The survey showed just how diverse our photographic interests are.

Landscape Photography was the most popular, followed by Seascape, but there was also strong interest in Macro, Street Photography, Sports and Action, Wildlife and Birds, Night Photography and Astrophotography.

There were also plenty of suggestions for places to visit, from local beaches, Tower Hill and Port Fairy through to the Grampians, Otways and Melbourne for street, architectural and zoo photography.

We're certainly not short of ideas.

Fog and Mist • Long Exposure • Wildlife • Reflections • Sunrise or Sunset • Industrial/Architecture

We also received some thoughtful comments about making sure we have a broad range of subjects that members with different levels of experience and confidence can tackle.

That's something the committee will take into consideration when planning future set subjects.

The leading workshop interests included:

The feedback about our weekly newsletter was particularly encouraging.

Every member who answered the satisfaction question rated the newsletter either Satisfied or Very Satisfied.

Members told us they particularly value the photography and editing tips, learning material, regular information and reminders about what is happening around the Club.

Making Our Articles Easier to Find

Our Weekly Updates are now available on the Warrnambool Camera Club website.

Over time, the newsletter has become much more than a list of Club announcements. We now have a growing collection of photography tips, Lightroom and Photoshop articles, camera guides, tutorials and other educational material.

The problem is that if you remember reading something useful three or six months ago, finding that particular article buried amongst old emails isn't particularly easy.

We want to fix that.

The newsletters now have a permanent home on the website, and we have developed a searchable Photography Resource Library that makes it much easier to find individual articles by topic.

Want to find that article about White Balance? Or Long Exposure Photography? Or something about Lightroom?

Rather than searching through months of emails, the aim is that you'll be able to go to the website and find it directly.

Previous Weekly Updates are also being progressively added to the online archive, so members will be able to browse earlier editions as well as find individual articles and tutorials.

The weekly email newsletter isn't going anywhere. You'll continue receiving it just as you do now.

The website simply gives all that useful material a permanent home where you can find it again when you need it.

It was also pleasing to see that members feel well informed about what is happening around the Club.

That's important because we're not looking to replace something that's already working.

The newsletter will continue to provide regular Club information, while the website will make our growing library of information easier to find and the private Facebook group will provide a more immediate, informal way for members to connect with one another.

📷 Convention Corner

Mastering Long Exposure Photography

Part 7: Exposure and the Histogram – Don’t Trust Your Screen

You’ve composed the photograph, carefully focused the camera, fitted your ND filter and made the exposure. The photograph appears on the back of the camera. It looks great.

But is it correctly exposed?

During his VAPS Convention presentation, Ian van der Wolde made an important point about something almost every photographer does: judging exposure by looking at the photograph on the camera’s rear screen.

The problem is that our eyes are easily fooled.

The histogram gives us much more useful information.

Why Your Camera Screen Can Mislead You

Imagine photographing a long exposure on a beach in bright daylight. You look at the rear LCD and the photograph appears quite dark.

Later that evening, you look at exactly the same photograph in a dark room and suddenly it appears bright.

The file hasn’t changed. Your viewing environment has.

That is why judging exposure purely by whether the photograph “looks right” on the rear LCD is not particularly reliable.

Meet the Histogram

The histogram is a graphical representation of the brightness values contained within your photograph.

The left side represents darker tones. The right side represents brighter tones. Everything between represents the tonal range captured in the image.

A histogram weighted towards the left does not automatically mean a photograph is underexposed, just as a histogram weighted towards the right does not automatically mean it is overexposed.

There is no single perfect histogram shape.

What matters is whether the histogram represents the scene and whether you have retained the information you need.

Watch the Edges

The areas that deserve particular attention are the extreme ends of the histogram.

If important information is pushed hard against the right edge, highlights may be clipped. Once highlight information is completely lost, recovering it later may be impossible.

At the opposite end, if important shadow detail is crushed against the left edge, those areas may contain very little usable information.

Why Ian Likes to “Expose to the Right”

Ian discussed a technique commonly known as ETTR – Expose To The Right.

The idea is to give the RAW file as much exposure as practical without clipping important highlights.

Digital sensors generally record cleaner information when given more light. If an image is significantly underexposed and you later brighten the shadows, noise can become much more obvious.

But there is an important qualification:

Expose to the right does not mean overexpose the photograph.

If important highlights are clipping, you have gone too far.

Why This Matters for Long Exposures

Long exposure photographs can contain enormous differences in brightness.

A sunset seascape may have a relatively bright sky while rocks in the foreground are becoming very dark. A pier at night may contain bright lights surrounded by deep shadows.

Instead of asking:

“Does this look bright enough?”

Ask:

“Where is the information in my file?”

Take a Test Exposure First

Ian recommended making a normal exposure before fitting a strong ND filter.

This allows you to check:

  • Composition
  • Focus
  • Exposure
  • Histogram
  • Highlights
  • Shadows

Once you are happy with that starting exposure, calculate the longer exposure required when the ND filter is added.

Get the photograph right first. Then make it long.

Highlight Warnings, RAW and One Important Limitation

Many cameras offer a highlight warning, sometimes affectionately known as the “blinkies”. Areas that may be overexposed flash on the rear display.

A tiny specular highlight on water or a streetlight clipping may not matter. A large section of an important sunset sky flashing probably does.

Ian also strongly recommended shooting RAW because it gives much greater flexibility when adjusting highlights, shadows, white balance and colour later.

One thing to remember is that the histogram displayed by the camera is generally based on the processed preview, not directly on the RAW data, so picture styles and contrast settings can influence what you see.

Treat the histogram as an extremely useful field guide rather than an infallible laboratory measurement.

Ian’s Basic Exposure Workflow

Compose → Focus → Make a Test Exposure → Check Histogram → Check Highlights → Adjust Exposure → Add ND Filter → Calculate Long Exposure → Shoot

That test photograph might take only a few seconds. It could save you from wasting ten minutes on an incorrectly exposed long exposure.

Try It This Week

Find a scene containing both bright and dark areas and take three photographs:

  • One deliberately underexposed
  • One at the exposure you think is correct
  • One deliberately overexposed

Now ignore how bright or dark the photographs appear on the rear screen and look at their histograms instead.

Notice how the information moves from left to right as exposure increases.

Then compare the files later in Lightroom or your preferred RAW processor.

Quick Tip

Don’t ask whether the photograph looks bright enough on the camera screen.

Ask whether you have captured the tonal information you need.

💻 Editing Corner

Lightroom Basics – Part 6

Noise Reduction Without Losing Detail

Noise is something every photographer encounters eventually, particularly when shooting at high ISO, in poor light, or when lifting dark areas during editing.

In Lightroom, noise generally appears in two forms:

  • Luminance noise – the grainy, speckled look that affects brightness
  • Colour noise – small unwanted red, green, blue or purple speckles

The challenge is not simply removing noise.

The real goal is to reduce the noise while keeping as much genuine detail as possible.

1. Zoom In Before You Start

Open the photograph in Lightroom Classic’s Develop module and zoom to around 100% or 1:1.

Noise reduction can be difficult to judge when the whole photograph is fitted to the screen.

Look particularly at dark backgrounds, shadows, smooth skies, skin, fur and feathers.

2. Two Ways to Reduce Noise

Lightroom gives you two main approaches.

Traditional noise reduction uses the sliders in the Detail panel.

AI Denoise uses Adobe’s newer processing to reduce noise while attempting to preserve fine detail.

It is worth understanding both rather than assuming one method is always best.

3. Start With Luminance Noise

The Luminance slider deals with the grainy brightness variations we usually think of as digital noise.

Move it gradually to the right and watch the grain begin to disappear.

The catch is that the photograph becomes smoother as you increase the setting.

Beginner rule: stop before feathers, fur, skin or fine texture start looking plastic.

4. Detail, Contrast and Colour Noise

The Detail control determines how much fine detail Lightroom tries to preserve.

Higher settings retain more detail but may leave more visible noise. Lower settings produce a smoother result but can remove fine texture.

Colour noise reduction deals with unwanted coloured speckles and is often easier to remove without damaging image detail.

5. Try AI Denoise

For suitable RAW files, Lightroom’s AI Denoise can produce very good results.

It is particularly useful for high ISO photographs where conventional luminance reduction would need to be pushed quite hard.

But the same rule applies: judge the final photograph, not simply how clean the background looks.

6. Don’t Remove Every Trace of Noise

This is one of the biggest beginner traps.

A small amount of fine noise is often far less objectionable than a photograph that has been smoothed until all the texture disappears.

A bird photograph with a little grain in the background but beautifully detailed feathers will usually look far better than completely smooth noise accompanied by waxy feathers.

7. Noise Reduction and Sharpening Work Together

Remember Part 5 of our Lightroom Basics series.

Sharpening and noise reduction both operate on fine image detail.

Too much sharpening can make noise more visible. Too much noise reduction can remove the detail you are trying to sharpen.

The two adjustments therefore need to be judged together.

A Simple Beginner Method

  • Zoom to 100%.
  • Open the Detail panel.
  • Identify whether the problem is grain, colour speckles or both.
  • Try Denoise if working with a suitable RAW file.
  • Otherwise increase Luminance gradually.
  • Adjust Detail only as much as necessary.
  • Check Colour noise reduction.
  • Recheck your sharpening.
  • Compare the photograph before and after.

Remember:

A little noise is usually better than a lot of lost detail.

Noise reduction should clean the photograph, not erase its texture.

📸 Photographer’s Tips

🌱 Beginner Tip – B009

Fill the Frame

If your subject is the reason for taking the photograph, make sure it has enough presence in the frame.

Before pressing the shutter, ask whether there is empty or distracting space that adds nothing to the photograph. If it is safe and practical, move closer or choose a longer focal length rather than relying on a heavy crop later.

Filling the frame does not mean every photograph must be tight. It simply means making a deliberate decision about how much space your subject needs.

📷 Intermediate Tip – I009

Use Reflections as Part of the Composition

When you find a reflection, do not treat it as an accidental extra. Decide how much of it belongs in the photograph.

A complete reflection can create strong symmetry, while cutting through a reflection deliberately can produce a more dynamic composition.

Move your camera higher or lower and watch how the relationship between the subject and its reflection changes. A small change in viewpoint can completely alter the balance of the image.

⭐ Advanced Tip – A009

Use Repetition, Then Break the Pattern

Repeated shapes, colours, lines or objects create visual rhythm and can make a photograph immediately satisfying to look at.

But repetition becomes even stronger when one element is different.

Look for the red chair among blue chairs, the one bird facing the other way, the single open window in a row of closed ones, or a person entering an otherwise regular pattern.

The repeated elements establish the structure. The break in the pattern gives the viewer somewhere to look.

📷 This Week’s Camera Challenge

One Subject, Three Interpretations

Choose one subject and make three photographs that look genuinely different from one another.

Try:

  • A wider environmental photograph showing where the subject is
  • A tighter composition concentrating on the subject itself
  • A detail or abstract interpretation that someone might not immediately recognise

You can use different focal lengths, camera heights, angles, apertures or distances, but the subject must remain the same.

The aim is to remind ourselves that finding a subject is only the beginning. The real creative decision is how we choose to show it.
