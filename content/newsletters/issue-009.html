Photography Walk for Wellness Celebrates Two Years

Two years ago, I started the Warrnambool Photography Walk for Wellness with a simple idea—to bring people together through photography, fresh air and good company.

Twenty-five monthly walks later, that simple idea has grown into a thriving Facebook community of more than 195 members, with photographers regularly travelling from across south-west Victoria and south-east South Australia to take part.

Saturday's anniversary walk attracted 15 photographers, who spent an hour exploring Warrnambool before regrouping over coffee and morning tea.

One of the things that makes the Walk for Wellness a little different is that, after a short welcome, everyone heads off in their own direction for around an hour. Rather than following a leader or photographing the same subjects, participants are encouraged to explore independently and photograph whatever captures their imagination. It's always fascinating to see how fifteen photographers can return from the same location with fifteen completely different collections of images.

The anniversary celebrations were made even more special thanks to Tania Jacobson who kindly organised a beautiful anniversary cake for everyone to enjoy after the walk. Thanks also to Vivian Carter who supplied the candle

The milestone also attracted welcome media attention, with a feature appearing in this week's Warrnambool Weekly and an interview on ABC South West Victoria with Jeremy Lee, where Tania Jacobson and I had the opportunity to share the story behind the Walk for Wellness.

To everyone who has joined us over the past two years, —thank you. Here's to many more walks, many more photographs and many more friendships.

📷 Convention Corner

Read Ethical Bird Photography Part 4

Ethical Bird Photography Series – Part 4

The Photographer's Greatest Tool Isn't a Longer Lens

When photographers talk about improving their bird photography, the conversation often turns to longer lenses, faster cameras or improved autofocus. While better equipment can certainly help, Kim Wormald suggested that the most valuable tool in any wildlife photographer's kit bag is something that costs nothing at all:

Patience.

Throughout her presentation, Kim returned to the same principle again and again. Rather than chasing birds, she allows the birds to decide whether they are comfortable with her presence. Instead of moving closer until they fly away, she waits. Instead of trying to force a photograph, she becomes part of the environment.

It is a simple philosophy, but one that fundamentally changes both the experience and the photographs.

Kim shared the story of photographing Hooded Plovers while lying quietly on the beach. She remained still for over an hour, watching the family go about their daily routine. Eventually, the tiny chicks wandered towards her of their own accord, providing intimate photographs that simply would not have been possible had she approached them.

The lesson was clear: sometimes the best way to get closer is not to move at all.

Understanding bird behaviour is equally important. Learning how different species feed, rest, preen, defend territory and interact with each other helps photographers anticipate moments before they happen. Rather than reacting to behaviour, experienced wildlife photographers begin to predict it.

Kim encouraged photographers to spend time watching birds without even raising the camera. Observe how they move through their habitat. Notice favourite perches, feeding areas and flight paths. Listen for alarm calls that may indicate a bird is becoming uncomfortable. The more time spent observing, the more natural opportunities present themselves.

She also reminded us that fieldcraft extends beyond the subject itself. Small adjustments in position can completely transform an image. Moving only a metre or two may replace a cluttered background with soft colour, eliminate distracting branches or improve the angle of the light. Often, changing your position is far more effective than changing your lens.

Perhaps the most reassuring message was that not every outing needs to end with a full memory card. Some days the birds simply won't cooperate. Other days the conditions aren't right. Spending time immersed in nature is never wasted, even if the camera stays quiet.

As Kim observed, the magic of bird photography comes from becoming part of the landscape rather than disturbing it. When birds accept your presence and continue behaving naturally, the photographs that follow are not only more authentic, they are often the ones you'll treasure most.

Photography Tip: The next time you're out photographing birds, challenge yourself to spend the first 15 minutes simply watching. Leave the camera by your side, study the birds' behaviour, and let the photographs come to you rather than chasing them.

Read Cropping for Better Composition

Although this guide demonstrates the process using Adobe Lightroom, the same principles apply in most editing software. Whether you use Lightroom, Photoshop, Capture One, ON1, Luminar or another editor, understanding why you crop is far more important than which software you use.

Lightroom Basics – Part 2

Cropping for Better Composition

Cropping is one of the simplest ways to strengthen a photograph. A careful crop can remove distractions, improve balance and make the main subject much clearer.

In the example accompanying this article, the original photograph contains a large amount of empty water and foreground. The tighter crop places greater emphasis on the rescue boat and crew while still leaving enough room around the action.

A Simple Cropping Process in Lightroom

1. Open the Crop tool

In Lightroom Classic, press R or select Crop Overlay from the Develop module.

2. Check the edges

Before changing the composition, look carefully around all four edges of the photograph.

Watch for:

  • Empty or unnecessary space
  • Partial objects entering the frame
  • Bright areas that attract attention
  • Distracting background elements
  • Too much foreground or sky

In this photograph, some of the empty water and foreground can be removed without losing anything important.

3. Straighten the photograph

Check whether the horizon is level.

Use the Angle slider, the Straighten tool, or Lightroom’s Auto option. A slightly tilted horizon can make an otherwise strong photograph look unfinished.

4. Choose an aspect ratio

The aspect ratio determines the shape of the finished photograph.

Common choices include:

  • Original to retain the camera’s original proportions
  • 3 × 2 for a standard camera format
  • 4 × 5 for many print and social-media uses
  • 1 × 1 for a square composition
  • 16 × 9 for a wide presentation or screen

Choose the ratio according to how the photograph will be displayed or printed.

5. Recompose gently

Drag the edges or corners of the crop frame inward.

Keep the main subject prominent, but avoid cropping so tightly that the photograph feels cramped. For moving subjects, leave some room in front of the direction of travel. This gives the action somewhere to move within the frame.

6. Compare before and after

Press the backslash key \\ to compare the edited photograph with the original.

Ask yourself:

  • Is the subject now easier to identify?
  • Have unnecessary areas been removed?
  • Does the photograph feel better balanced?
  • Is there still enough room around the action?
  • Has the crop removed anything important?

Beginner Warning

Cropping closer does not automatically improve a photograph. An overly tight crop can remove context, reduce image quality and leave the subject feeling boxed in.

Small, deliberate changes usually work better than dramatic crops.

Final Check

A successful crop should make the subject clearer and the edges of the frame quieter.

The editing should not be the first thing the viewer notices. Their attention should go directly to the photograph’s main subject.

Useful shortcuts

R — Open the Crop tool X — Change the crop between horizontal and vertical orientation \ — Compare the edited photograph with the original

📸 Photographer's Tips

🌱 Beginner Tip

Hold Your Camera Steady

Even the sharpest lens can't compensate for camera movement.

Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart, tuck your elbows into your body and gently squeeze the shutter rather than jabbing at it. If you're shooting at slower shutter speeds, lean against a wall, rest the camera on a fence or use a tripod whenever possible.

Good camera technique costs nothing but can noticeably improve the sharpness of your photographs.

📷 Intermediate Tip

Use Natural Frames

A frame within your photograph can draw attention to your subject and add depth to the image.

Look for doorways, windows, tree branches, arches or even gaps in foliage that naturally surround your subject. The frame shouldn't overpower the image—it should simply help guide the viewer's eye to what matters most.

Before taking the shot, ask yourself whether the frame strengthens the story or merely adds clutter.

⭐ Advanced Tip

Balance the Visual Weight

Not every photograph needs perfect symmetry, but every strong image needs balance.

Large, bright or colourful objects naturally attract the eye more than smaller or darker ones. If your main subject sits on one side of the frame, look for another element that subtly balances the composition without competing for attention.

Understanding visual weight helps create images that feel harmonious and encourages the viewer to spend more time exploring the photograph.

🇦🇺 Bob & Kerrie Conquer the Tip of Australia

Bob and Kerrie have safely completed their camping adventure to the Tip of Cape York and are now enjoying a well-earned break in the beautiful Atherton Tablelands before continuing the next leg of their adventure.

After spending 18 nights in a tent, they're now back enjoying the comforts of the caravan. As Kerrie put it, "the caravan is definitely luxury compared to the tent!" Bob, however, couldn't resist pointing out that the tent may actually have been a little more spacious! 😊

They're in no particular hurry, planning to meander their way across Queensland and the Northern Territory over the coming month before arriving in Alice Springs in September. Knowing Bob and Kerrie, there will be plenty of spectacular scenery, interesting stories and photographic opportunities discovered along the way.

Bob also shared one observation from the journey to the Tip. Apparently, tackling Cape York's rugged tracks is much easier if your vehicle has three pedals and two gear levers.

Unfortunately, his four-wheel drive came with considerably more electronics than that, and he admitted to being more than a little envious of another traveller's simpler setup, which seemed to tackle everything with ease.

Perhaps it's proof that, every now and then, less technology really is more... especially if you're just a little technically challenged! 😊

Safe travels, Bob and Kerrie. We look forward to following your journey over the coming weeks and, of course, seeing plenty of photographs as you make your way towards Alice Springs.

🇪🇺 Craig & Sharon Continue Their European Adventure

Craig and Sharon Homberg are certainly making the most of their overseas adventure.

So far their travels have taken them through London, Ireland and Frankfurt.

From the bustling streets of London to the picturesque Rhine Valley at Eltville in Germany, their journey has already produced some wonderful memories and, no doubt, plenty of photographs.

By the time you read this, they'll be back in London for a few days before heading off on the next leg of their adventure to Iceland, where we're sure there'll be no shortage of spectacular landscapes to photograph.

We hope you both continue to enjoy your travels and look forward to seeing your favourite images and hearing a few stories when you return home.
