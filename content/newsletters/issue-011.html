📷 World Photography Day

Wednesday, 19 August

World Photography Day is celebrated every year on 19 August, recognising one of the most significant moments in the development of photography.

Why do photographers celebrate 19 August?

On 19 August 1839, details of the daguerreotype photographic process were publicly presented in France, helping make practical photography available to the wider world.

Photography has changed enormously since then.

Glass plates became film. Film became digital. Cameras became smaller, faster and considerably smarter. Today, almost everyone carries a capable camera in their pocket.

Yet the basic purpose remains remarkably similar.

A photograph allows us to preserve a moment and show somebody else how we saw the world.

⏱️ Getting Beyond 30 Seconds

Some of the best discussion actually continued over dinner afterwards.

One topic that generated plenty of conversation was very long exposures.

Some photographers were discussing exposures running for several minutes, including exposures around eight minutes, while others found that their cameras appeared to stop at around 30 seconds.

That led to a useful discussion about Bulb mode, remote releases and using phones connected to cameras as remote triggers.

For many cameras, 30 seconds is simply the longest shutter speed available through the normal shutter-speed settings. Switching to Bulb mode, or a dedicated long-exposure mode depending on the camera, allows the shutter to remain open considerably longer.

Using a remote trigger or compatible phone app also means the photographer can start and finish the exposure without touching the camera and introducing movement.

Once exposures start running for several minutes, a phone also has another very useful function: a timer! 😊

It was a good reminder that workshops don't necessarily finish when the cameras are packed away. Sometimes just as much is learnt afterwards when everyone sits down and starts asking:

"How did you do that?"

📷 Convention Corner

Ethical Bird Photography Series - Part 5

Photography That Makes a Difference

As photographers, we often think about creating images that impress judges, earn awards or look great on the wall.

Kim Wormald challenged us to think about another purpose for our photographs: Can our images help protect the wildlife we love to photograph?

Read Part 5: Photography That Makes a Difference

Throughout her presentation, Kim shared numerous examples of how her bird photography has contributed to conservation efforts across Australia.

Her photographs have been used by wildlife organisations, conservation groups, researchers and advocacy campaigns to educate the public, document threatened species and promote habitat protection.

Sometimes a single image can tell a story that words struggle to convey.

Photographs of endangered Hooded Plovers encourage beachgoers to respect nesting areas. Images of banded birds provide valuable information to researchers tracking migration and longevity.

Pictures taken after bushfires document both the devastation and the remarkable resilience of Australia's wildlife as habitats begin to recover.

Kim also spoke about the responsibility photographers have when sharing images.

While it is exciting to discover a rare species, publicly revealing its exact location can unintentionally attract large numbers of photographers and birdwatchers, placing additional pressure on an already vulnerable bird.

Waiting until the bird has moved on, or simply omitting the precise location, is often the more responsible choice.

Another important message was that conservation photography is not always about beautiful images.

Some of Kim's most powerful photographs document the darker side of wildlife conservation, including birds injured by fishing line, the impacts of habitat loss and the consequences of human activity.

These images may be confronting, but they help start conversations that can lead to meaningful change.

For most of us, conservation photography doesn't require travelling to remote wilderness or working with research organisations. It can begin much closer to home.

Photograph the birds in your local reserve throughout the seasons. Record unusual visitors. Share images that celebrate native habitats. Support local conservation projects with your photographs. Submit observations to citizen science programs.

Every image has the potential to contribute to a broader understanding and appreciation of our natural environment.

Perhaps the greatest lesson from Kim's presentation was that wildlife photography is about far more than collecting images.

It is about building a connection with nature.

When we spend time quietly observing birds, learning their behaviour and respecting their space, we become ambassadors for the wildlife around us.

Our photographs begin to tell honest stories, stories that inspire others to appreciate, protect and care for the remarkable birds that share our landscapes.

A stunning photograph may capture someone's attention for a moment.

A photograph with purpose can change the way they see the natural world.

Photography Tip: Next time you're out with your camera, think beyond the individual image.

Ask yourself: "What story about this bird, its habitat or its conservation could this photograph help tell?"

Sometimes the most valuable photograph isn't the most dramatic. It's the one that encourages someone else to care.

💻 Editing Corner

Lightroom Basics - Part 4

White Balance Made Easy

Have you ever opened a photograph in Lightroom and thought the colours just didn't look right? Perhaps an indoor photograph looks too orange, a shaded scene looks too blue, or skin tones simply don't look natural.

This is usually where White Balance comes in.

Read White Balance Made Easy

White balance is Lightroom's way of correcting the colour cast created by different types of light.

The good news is that you do not need to understand colour temperature in great detail to make useful corrections.

1. Start with the Photograph

Before touching any sliders, ask yourself:

  • Does the photograph look too blue?
  • Does it look too yellow or orange?
  • Is there a green or magenta colour cast?
  • Do whites actually look white?
  • Do skin tones look believable?

The aim is not necessarily to make every photograph completely neutral. Sometimes warm evening light or cool early-morning light is part of what makes the photograph work.

2. Find White Balance in Lightroom

In Lightroom Classic, open the Develop module and look near the top of the Basic panel.

You will see:

  • WB
  • Temp
  • Tint

Lightroom can use the camera's original white balance with As Shot, or calculate its own interpretation using Auto.

RAW files also provide additional preset choices such as Daylight, Cloudy, Shade, Tungsten, Fluorescent and Flash.

Try Auto first.

Sometimes that may be all the photograph needs.

3. Use the Temperature Slider

The Temp slider controls the photograph's overall warm-to-cool balance.

  • Move left to make the photograph cooler and more blue.
  • Move right to make it warmer and more yellow or golden.

Make small adjustments.

If an indoor photograph looks strongly orange, move Temp slightly left. If a shaded photograph looks unnaturally blue, move it slightly right.

4. Use Tint Only If You Need It

The Tint slider deals mainly with the green-to-magenta balance.

  • Move left to add green.
  • Move right to add magenta.

For many photographs, you may hardly need to touch Tint at all.

Try the Eyedropper

Lightroom also includes the White Balance Selector, normally shown as an eyedropper.

Select it and click an area in the photograph that should be neutral.

A neutral area could be:

  • Grey.
  • White with visible detail.
  • A genuinely colourless surface.

Lightroom then adjusts Temperature and Tint to neutralise that area.

This can be an excellent starting point, but still look at the entire photograph afterwards. The technically neutral result is not always the most visually pleasing one.

RAW Gives You More Flexibility

White balance is especially forgiving when working with a RAW file.

Lightroom provides more white balance options for RAW photographs than for JPEGs, and RAW processing gives considerably more latitude for changing the colour balance after the photograph has been taken.

Don't Automatically Remove the Mood

Imagine a sunset photographed in beautiful warm light.

A technically neutral white balance could remove much of that warmth and leave the photograph looking flat.

Likewise, the blue feeling of an early winter morning may be completely appropriate.

White balance should correct unwanted colour, not automatically remove the colour of the light.

A Simple Beginner Method

  1. Try As Shot.
  2. Try Auto.
  3. Try the Eyedropper.
  4. Adjust Temp until the photograph looks believable.
  5. Adjust Tint only if there is an obvious green or magenta cast.
  6. Compare the result with the original.

Then choose the version that best represents the scene.

Final Check

Before moving on to the next stage of your edit, ask:

  • Do whites look believable?
  • Do people have natural-looking skin?
  • Has an unwanted blue, orange, green or magenta cast disappeared?
  • Have I accidentally removed the atmosphere of the original light?
  • Does the photograph simply look right?

If the answer is yes, stop adjusting.

Remember

White balance is not about making every photograph neutral. It is about making the colour believable.

Small adjustments are normally all that is required.

📸 Photographer's Tips

🌱 Beginner Tip

Understand What Aperture Does

Aperture controls how much light passes through your lens, but it also affects how much of your photograph appears in focus.

A wide aperture, such as f/2.8, can help blur the background and isolate your subject. A smaller aperture, such as f/8 or f/11, can keep more of the scene acceptably sharp.

Try photographing the same subject at several apertures without changing your position. Comparing the results is one of the easiest ways to understand how aperture changes the look of a photograph.

📷 Intermediate Tip

Build Your Image in Layers

Strong photographs often have more than just a subject and a background.

Look for opportunities to create foreground, middle ground and background elements within the same composition.

These layers can give a two-dimensional photograph a much stronger sense of depth.

The important part is that each layer contributes something.

⭐ Advanced Tip

Create Separation Between Elements

When two important objects overlap or touch awkwardly, they can visually merge and make the composition harder to read.

Before pressing the shutter, look carefully at the relationships between elements.

A small change in camera position may only require moving a few centimetres, but that small movement can make the final composition considerably cleaner and more deliberate.
